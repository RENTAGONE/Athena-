**Production message:** 13 of 20  |  **Status:** authored working draft

**Scope:** 12 higher-developing instructional units; two per discipline. This batch extends Message 12 and does not complete any discipline.

## Learning workflow and evidence contract
ATHENA assigns an objective, prerequisite set and evidence contract. The learner selects reputable external sources, records provenance, studies independently, then returns with a closed-book explanation and an application artifact or supervised demonstration. The evaluator scores correctness, reasoning, transfer, source quality, uncertainty and safety. A structured update is proposed for review; the learner previews and explicitly approves any persistent commit. Reading, attendance, AI-generated explanations or a single quiz never automatically award mastery.

## Unit format
Each unit below is a curriculum assignment, not a substitute for a qualified coach, clinician, instructor or guardian. Workload estimates are planning aids, not deadlines. Adapt for accessibility, available tools and learner context. If a task is unsafe, inaccessible or not feasible, use the stated alternative evidence route and document the substitution.

## BODY-ADV-001 — Exercise physiology: energy systems, intensity and adaptation
**Discipline:** BODY  |  **Level:** advanced_developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 4–6 hours plus observation across several ordinary activity sessions
**Prerequisites:** BODY-DEV-001; BODY-DEV-002; ATH-DEV-001; basic graph reading

**Learning outcomes**
- explain at a broad level how ATP supplies immediate cellular energy
- compare the overlapping roles of phosphagen, glycolytic and oxidative energy pathways without treating them as isolated switches
- interpret how intensity and duration influence perceived effort, breathing and recovery
- distinguish an acute response from a training adaptation
- identify why individual measurements and wearable estimates have limitations

**Core model**
Muscle contraction requires ATP. Because stored ATP is limited, the body continually resynthesises it through interacting energy pathways. Phosphagen contribution is rapid and supports very brief high-power efforts; glycolytic pathways contribute substantially during hard efforts lasting longer; oxidative metabolism supports sustained work and also contributes during shorter efforts. These systems overlap rather than switch on one at a time. Relative contribution depends on intensity, duration, training status, recovery, nutrition, environment and individual variation. Acute responses (such as increased ventilation) occur during or soon after activity; adaptation develops over repeated exposure and recovery. A simplified energy-system chart is a teaching model, not a precise personal measurement.

**Worked example**
A short acceleration, a repeated 20-second shuttle and a steady walk all require ATP. The first places a high demand on rapid energy supply; repeated shuttles add glycolytic and oxidative contributions as repetitions accumulate; the walk relies predominantly on oxidative metabolism. None uses only one pathway. A learner should not infer exact percentages from how tired they feel.

**Method / procedure**
1. Name the activity and its duration pattern.
2. Describe the intensity demand using observable context, not an invented laboratory value.
3. Explain which pathways are likely to contribute most and why, while acknowledging overlap.
4. Separate immediate response from adaptation over weeks.
5. List confounders and measurement limits.
6. Use observations to improve a training question, not to diagnose health or prescribe maximal effort.

**Guided practice**
Sort six activities (single jump, 5-second sprint, 30-second hard effort, 3-minute game, 20-minute easy cycle, repeated short sprints) by likely dominant contribution. Explain why the categories overlap. Draw a timeline showing how repeated efforts change recovery demand.

**Independent evidence task**
Create a two-page explainer with one activity comparison table, one labelled energy-pathway diagram, and a short observation log from ordinary, non-maximal activity. Include a paragraph explaining why fatigue is not a direct readout of one energy system. Use at least two reputable physiology/education sources and cite the exact sections.

**Assessment rubric**
1. ATP and pathway accuracy
2. reasoning about duration and intensity
3. acute response versus adaptation
4. quality of evidence and source provenance
5. limitations and safe interpretation
**Scoring:** score each dimension 0–3 with a written rationale (maximum 15). A typical developing-level target is 11/15, but safety-critical units require all safety conditions to be met. A score is evidence about this task, not automatic mastery.

**Common misconceptions**
- Energy systems do not operate as three sequential gears.
- Lactate is not simply a waste product that explains all soreness.
- Feeling exhausted does not identify which pathway supplied ATP.
- A training adaptation is not guaranteed by one hard session.

**Transfer links**
Connects BODY homeostasis to ATH training design and PSY interpretation of subjective effort. The same acute-versus-adaptation distinction applies to learning and practice.

**Safety, ethics and scope**
No maximal testing, breath-holding, dehydration, deliberate exhaustion or unsupervised high-intensity protocols. This is conceptual physiology, not a personal training prescription. Stop activity for pain, dizziness, faintness or unusual breathing difficulty and tell a trusted adult.

**Retention and reassessment**
After study, explain the three pathways from memory, then interpret a new activity example several days later. Revisit errors by concept; do not treat one recall score as mastery.

## BODY-ADV-002 — Health information, epidemiology and evidence-based public-health reasoning
**Discipline:** BODY  |  **Level:** advanced_developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 4–5 hours
**Prerequisites:** BODY-DEV-002; basic fractions, percentages and graph reading; PSY-DEV-001 or equivalent source-evaluation skill

**Learning outcomes**
- distinguish incidence, prevalence, risk and rate in plain language
- explain why population-level patterns do not predict an individual outcome
- identify confounding, selection bias and measurement bias in a simple claim
- compare relative and absolute risk statements
- write a cautious evidence summary that separates observation from recommendation

**Core model**
Epidemiology studies patterns and causes of health-related states in populations. Incidence concerns new cases over a period; prevalence concerns existing cases in a population at a point or period, depending on definition. Risk is a probability over a specified time and population. Observational associations can generate hypotheses but may be affected by confounding, selection and measurement. Randomised studies can reduce some biases but do not answer every question and may not generalise. Public-health reasoning combines evidence, context, uncertainty, equity, feasibility and potential harms. Population statistics must not be used to diagnose a person.

**Worked example**
A headline says a habit “doubles risk.” If the baseline probability were 1 in 1,000 over a defined period, doubling would be 2 in 1,000, not 50%. The absolute difference is 1 in 1,000. The actual interpretation still depends on study quality, population and time horizon.

**Method / procedure**
1. Rewrite the claim as a precise question with population, exposure, outcome and time.
2. Identify whether the measure is incidence, prevalence, relative risk, absolute risk or another statistic.
3. Inspect study design, sample, measurement and comparison group.
4. Look for confounders and selection effects.
5. Separate association from causation.
6. State what the evidence supports, what remains uncertain and who should make decisions.

**Guided practice**
Use a fictional dataset of 10,000 people: 10 experience an outcome in group A and 20 in group B over the same stated period. Calculate absolute risks and the risk ratio; explain why this alone does not prove the exposure caused the difference.

**Independent evidence task**
Prepare a 700–900 word public-health evidence brief on a non-sensitive, broadly educational topic such as handwashing or road-safety behaviour. Include definitions, one correctly interpreted statistic, source-quality notes, uncertainty and a non-prescriptive conclusion. Do not analyse private medical data.

**Assessment rubric**
1. correct use of epidemiological terms
2. quantitative interpretation
3. bias/confounding analysis
4. source quality and provenance
5. cautious, non-diagnostic communication
**Scoring:** score each dimension 0–3 with a written rationale (maximum 15). A typical developing-level target is 11/15, but safety-critical units require all safety conditions to be met. A score is evidence about this task, not automatic mastery.

**Common misconceptions**
- A larger relative risk may correspond to a small absolute difference.
- A correlation does not establish causation.
- A population average does not predict an individual with certainty.
- A study being peer reviewed does not make it infallible.

**Transfer links**
Supports FIN risk interpretation, PSY bias analysis and BIZ market research. The distinction between absolute and relative change is useful in any decision involving percentages.

**Safety, ethics and scope**
Educational evidence literacy only. Do not collect identifiable health information, diagnose anyone, recommend treatment or replace advice from qualified health professionals.

**Retention and reassessment**
Recalculate a fresh fictional example after a delay and explain the same finding to a non-specialist without losing its uncertainty.

## ATH-ADV-001 — Training design: progression, load management and recovery
**Discipline:** ATH  |  **Level:** advanced_developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 4–6 hours design work plus a supervised, low-risk practice cycle
**Prerequisites:** ATH-DEV-001; ATH-DEV-002; BODY-ADV-001; basic arithmetic and table reading

**Learning outcomes**
- distinguish external workload from internal response
- explain progressive overload, specificity, variation and recovery
- design a conservative practice microcycle around an existing activity schedule
- use a simple session reflection without treating it as medical measurement
- identify when to reduce, modify or stop a session

**Core model**
Training is a planned exposure to a task intended to develop capacity or skill. External load describes the work performed (for example, duration, distance or repetitions); internal response describes how demanding it felt and how the person responded. Neither measure is complete alone. Progression should be gradual, specific to the goal and balanced with recovery, sleep, ordinary life demands and enjoyment. A microcycle is a short planning block, often about a week, but real schedules should fit the learner rather than force a template. Variation can reduce monotony; it should not become random complexity. More work is not automatically better.

**Worked example**
A learner already plays football twice weekly. Adding hard sprint sessions on both adjacent days may reduce movement quality and recovery. A better plan may place technical practice on one day, allow recovery around the existing games and use a short low-intensity mobility or ball-control session only if the learner feels well and has adult/coach approval.

**Method / procedure**
1. Define one goal and one observable indicator.
2. Map fixed commitments, rest opportunities and constraints.
3. Choose a small amount of goal-specific practice.
4. Record external work and a simple subjective response separately.
5. Plan recovery and a fallback version.
6. Review quality, enjoyment, soreness and schedule fit before changing one variable.

**Guided practice**
Compare two fictional weekly schedules. Identify stacked high-demand days, missing recovery and sessions that do not match the goal. Revise one schedule while preserving school, sleep and existing commitments.

**Independent evidence task**
Submit a one-week practice microcycle for a familiar, low-risk activity, with goal, session purpose, duration, easy alternative, rest plan, stop rules and a post-session reflection template. Have a trusted adult or qualified coach review any physical implementation.

**Assessment rubric**
1. goal and specificity alignment
2. realistic progression and recovery
3. separation of external/internal load
4. contingency and stop rules
5. reflection and revision quality
**Scoring:** score each dimension 0–3 with a written rationale (maximum 15). A typical developing-level target is 11/15, but safety-critical units require all safety conditions to be met. A score is evidence about this task, not automatic mastery.

**Common misconceptions**
- A fixed percentage increase is not universally safe for every person or activity.
- Soreness is not a reliable score of training quality.
- A wearable readiness score cannot replace judgement.
- Rest is part of a plan, not evidence of laziness.

**Transfer links**
Connects to BODY adaptation, WORK project capacity planning and PSY habit design. Use the same plan–observe–adjust loop for learning projects.

**Safety, ethics and scope**
No maximal tests, unsupervised heavy lifting, forced weight targets or training through pain. Youth training should be age-appropriate and reviewed by a responsible adult/coach. Health concerns go to a qualified professional.

**Retention and reassessment**
Review the plan after one cycle using evidence, not guilt. Preserve the original plan and annotate why changes were made.

## ATH-ADV-002 — Game analysis: principles of play, constraints and performance review
**Discipline:** ATH  |  **Level:** advanced_developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 4–6 hours including match observation
**Prerequisites:** ATH-DEV-002; basic rules and positional vocabulary; ability to make a simple timeline or event log

**Learning outcomes**
- analyse a team phase using width, depth, support, balance and penetration as contextual principles
- distinguish tactical decision quality from execution outcome
- code a small sample of game events consistently
- identify how constraints change available decisions
- produce a fair, evidence-based review with alternative explanations

**Core model**
Game analysis asks what happened, in what context, and why an action may have been selected. Principles such as width, depth, support, balance, penetration and compactness are lenses, not universal commands. A pass backward may preserve possession or reset an attack; a forward pass may be forced and unsafe. Performance review should separate perception, decision, execution and outcome. A small sample is vulnerable to selection bias and observer interpretation. Video review requires consent and respect for privacy.

**Worked example**
A player receives facing their own goal under pressure and passes backward. A result-only review might label it negative. A contextual review asks whether forward lanes were closed, whether a teammate offered support, whether the backward pass retained possession and whether the team could then switch play.

**Method / procedure**
1. Choose one phase or question, not “analyse everything”.
2. Define observable event codes before watching.
3. Record context: location, pressure, options and team shape.
4. Separate what was seen from what is inferred.
5. Review decision and execution independently.
6. Offer one plausible alternative and state what evidence would distinguish it.

**Guided practice**
Watch a short, permitted clip or observe a live game. Code ten possessions for support angle, pressure and next action. Recode a sample later to check whether definitions were applied consistently.

**Independent evidence task**
Create a 10–15 event analysis sheet, a simple team-shape diagram and a 500-word review. Include coding definitions, at least two counterexamples and a note on sample limitations. Use only footage you are allowed to view and share.

**Assessment rubric**
1. clarity of event definitions
2. contextual tactical reasoning
3. decision/execution separation
4. consistency and fairness
5. privacy and limitations
**Scoring:** score each dimension 0–3 with a written rationale (maximum 15). A typical developing-level target is 11/15, but safety-critical units require all safety conditions to be met. A score is evidence about this task, not automatic mastery.

**Common misconceptions**
- A single clip rarely proves a player is consistently good or bad.
- Possession and progression are not identical.
- The best action depends on teammates, opponents, space, score and time.
- Hindsight makes options look easier than they were.

**Transfer links**
Connects to PSY decision-making, AEST information visualisation and LEAD feedback. Evidence-based review is useful in any team or project.

**Safety, ethics and scope**
No public shaming, hostile ranking or non-consensual recording. Do not infer character from a mistake; discuss actions and context respectfully.

**Retention and reassessment**
Repeat the same coding scheme on a different match and compare consistency before changing the framework.

## PERF-ADV-001 — Diction, articulation and expressive speech for an audience
**Discipline:** PERF  |  **Level:** advanced_developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 3–5 hours over several short sessions
**Prerequisites:** PERF-DEV-001; PERF-DEV-002; COMM foundational speaking skills

**Learning outcomes**
- distinguish articulation, diction, pace, phrasing and emphasis
- adapt speech clarity to audience, room and recording conditions
- use a controlled rehearsal loop to improve intelligibility
- avoid over-articulation, forced accents and vocal strain
- evaluate listener comprehension separately from personal preference

**Core model**
Intelligibility is produced by several interacting variables: consonant clarity, vowel shaping, pace, pauses, phrasing, volume, room acoustics and microphone placement. “Clear” does not mean exaggerated mouth movement or adopting an accent. Effective delivery is audience- and context-dependent. A recording can reveal patterns that the speaker does not notice, but microphone, compression and room conditions alter the sound. Rehearsal should target one variable at a time and preserve natural, comfortable vocal production.

**Worked example**
A fast explanation contains accurate information but listeners miss key terms. Slowing every word may sound unnatural. Instead, the speaker marks meaning groups, gives technical terms a little space, articulates word endings and checks comprehension with a listener.

**Method / procedure**
1. Identify the audience, purpose and likely unfamiliar terms.
2. Mark phrase boundaries and words that carry meaning.
3. Record a comfortable baseline in a quiet space.
4. Choose one target: pace, articulation, pause or emphasis.
5. Re-record under similar conditions.
6. Ask a listener to paraphrase the message before giving feedback.
7. Keep changes that improve comprehension without strain.

**Guided practice**
Read the same 30-second passage twice: first naturally, then with deliberate phrase boundaries and emphasis. Ask a listener to write the three main points they understood. Compare comprehension rather than judging the voice aesthetically.

**Independent evidence task**
Submit the passage, marked script, two short recordings (or a supervised live comparison), listener paraphrase, self-analysis and one revision. If recording is not appropriate, use a live listener protocol.

**Assessment rubric**
1. audience and purpose fit
2. articulation and phrasing control
3. natural, comfortable delivery
4. quality of feedback evidence
5. reflective revision
**Scoring:** score each dimension 0–3 with a written rationale (maximum 15). A typical developing-level target is 11/15, but safety-critical units require all safety conditions to be met. A score is evidence about this task, not automatic mastery.

**Common misconceptions**
- A louder voice is not necessarily clearer.
- An accent is not a defect to erase.
- Tongue twisters alone do not teach meaningful delivery.
- A recording is evidence under particular conditions, not an objective verdict on a person.

**Transfer links**
Links to COMM presentations, PERF interpretation and LEAD feedback. The listener-paraphrase test also improves written instructions.

**Safety, ethics and scope**
No shouting, forced accents, extreme pitch, breath manipulation or practice through discomfort. Protect privacy when recording and obtain consent from everyone recorded.

**Retention and reassessment**
Use a fresh passage after a delay to test transfer beyond memorising one script.

## PERF-ADV-002 — Performance production: rehearsal systems, recording and critique
**Discipline:** PERF  |  **Level:** advanced_developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 5–8 hours across a small production cycle
**Prerequisites:** PERF-DEV-002; PERF-ADV-001; basic file organisation and consent awareness

**Learning outcomes**
- plan a short performance from brief to delivery
- distinguish artistic intention from technical production constraints
- capture usable audio/video with a simple repeatable setup
- give and receive behaviour-specific critique
- manage versions, rights, consent and reflection

**Core model**
A small production moves through brief, concept, rehearsal, capture, review, revision and delivery. The brief defines audience, purpose, length, format and constraints. Technical quality is not the same as artistic quality: a clean recording can still lack meaning, while a compelling performance may have imperfect equipment. A controlled setup (same room, approximate distance, consistent device orientation) improves comparison. Critique should describe observable behaviour and its effect, not attack identity or taste. Media rights and consent apply to scripts, music, images and people.

**Worked example**
A 60-second spoken performance is recorded in three takes. Take one has clear delivery but weak pacing; take two has stronger pacing but clipping; take three preserves pacing and adjusts microphone distance. The production note records what changed and why rather than claiming take three is universally best.

**Method / procedure**
1. Write a one-paragraph brief and success criteria.
2. Check rights, permissions and privacy before capture.
3. Create a simple shot/audio plan and file naming convention.
4. Rehearse the performance separately from equipment tests.
5. Capture a baseline and review against the brief.
6. Change one or two variables, retain earlier versions and compare.
7. Export a final copy and a short production log.

**Guided practice**
Produce a 30–60 second original performance using only materials you have permission to use. Capture two versions under similar conditions and ask two listeners for specific feedback.

**Independent evidence task**
Deliver the brief, script/score, rights-and-consent checklist, version log, final performance, two feedback notes and a reflection on artistic choices versus technical limitations.

**Assessment rubric**
1. brief and audience alignment
2. rehearsal/production workflow
3. technical capture consistency
4. specific and respectful critique
5. rights, consent and version provenance
**Scoring:** score each dimension 0–3 with a written rationale (maximum 15). A typical developing-level target is 11/15, but safety-critical units require all safety conditions to be met. A score is evidence about this task, not automatic mastery.

**Common misconceptions**
- More editing does not guarantee a better performance.
- Feedback from two people is useful but not representative of every audience.
- A polished effect can hide unclear content.
- Using online media does not automatically grant permission.

**Transfer links**
Connects WORK version control, AEST design systems and ART iteration. The production log is a reusable project-management artifact.

**Safety, ethics and scope**
Use safe volume levels, comfortable posture and ordinary device setups. Do not publish faces, voices, locations or personal details without explicit permission and guardian guidance where appropriate.

**Retention and reassessment**
Repeat the workflow with a different brief and compare whether the process—not merely the specific performance—has improved.

## AEST-ADV-001 — Typography systems, responsive layout and interface consistency
**Discipline:** AEST  |  **Level:** advanced_developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 5–7 hours
**Prerequisites:** AEST-DEV-001; AEST-DEV-002; basic digital layout tool or paper prototyping

**Learning outcomes**
- select type roles using hierarchy, readability and context
- build a small spacing and layout system rather than arbitrary one-off values
- explain responsive reflow and content priority across screen sizes
- design reusable components with clear states
- test readability and consistency with users or structured inspection

**Core model**
A design system is a small set of reusable decisions: type scale, spacing scale, colour roles, grid rules, component patterns and interaction states. Typography hierarchy comes from contrast in size, weight, spacing and placement—not decoration alone. Responsive design adapts content to available space; it is not merely shrinking a desktop layout. Components should communicate purpose and state consistently. A token system reduces drift but can become rigid if it prevents content from fitting the task. Accessibility and legibility are requirements, not polish.

**Worked example**
A mobile page has a large hero heading, several cards and a long navigation row. Simply shrinking the desktop view makes text cramped. A responsive redesign moves navigation into a compact pattern, stacks cards, reduces decorative space and preserves the primary action and reading order.

**Method / procedure**
1. Identify content hierarchy and primary user task.
2. Define a limited type scale and spacing scale.
3. Set grid/container rules for narrow and wide layouts.
4. Create reusable components and document their states.
5. Test long labels, missing images, keyboard focus and zoom/reflow.
6. Revise tokens only when evidence shows a repeated problem.

**Guided practice**
Create one page at narrow-phone and wide-desktop widths using the same content. Use no more than six type roles and a small spacing scale. Test a long heading and a translated or expanded label.

**Independent evidence task**
Submit annotated screenshots or paper layouts, token table, component inventory, responsive comparison and a short usability test with at least two participants or a clearly documented heuristic review if participants are unavailable.

**Assessment rubric**
1. hierarchy and typography rationale
2. responsive content behaviour
3. consistency and component states
4. readability/accessibility checks
5. evidence-led revision
**Scoring:** score each dimension 0–3 with a written rationale (maximum 15). A typical developing-level target is 11/15, but safety-critical units require all safety conditions to be met. A score is evidence about this task, not automatic mastery.

**Common misconceptions**
- A design system is not just a colour palette.
- Responsive design is not one breakpoint or a smaller desktop screenshot.
- A trendy typeface does not compensate for poor hierarchy.
- Consistency does not require every component to look identical.

**Transfer links**
Connects AEST colour/contrast to WORK reusable processes and CYBER interface trust cues. Token thinking also applies to document templates.

**Safety, ethics and scope**
Do not collect unnecessary user data during testing. Obtain consent, avoid exposing personal information and provide accessible alternatives for participants.

**Retention and reassessment**
Rebuild one component in a different project using the same tokens, then note which rules transferred and which required context-specific changes.

## AEST-ADV-002 — Information design: charts, dashboards and truthful visual encoding
**Discipline:** AEST  |  **Level:** advanced_developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 5–7 hours
**Prerequisites:** AEST-DEV-001; AEST-DEV-002; basic percentages, averages and table reading

**Learning outcomes**
- match chart form to data type and comparison task
- identify misleading axes, scales, aggregation and omitted context
- design labels, legends and annotations for interpretation
- distinguish descriptive display from causal explanation
- test whether a reader can extract the intended message accurately

**Core model**
Information design maps data to visual channels such as position, length, area, colour and shape. Position and length are generally easier to compare accurately than area or colour intensity. A chart should preserve the data’s meaning, show units and time windows, and make relevant denominators visible. Truncated axes can exaggerate differences; dual axes can imply relationships; averages can hide distributions. A dashboard should prioritise decisions and context rather than maximise the number of charts. A visualisation does not establish causation by itself.

**Worked example**
A bar chart compares two values, 48 and 52, but its vertical axis starts at 47. The difference appears dramatic. Showing a zero baseline for bars or clearly explaining a justified non-zero scale prevents the visual from overstating the gap.

**Method / procedure**
1. State the question the reader needs answered.
2. Identify data types, units, denominator and time period.
3. Choose a chart whose visual encoding fits the comparison.
4. Show source, scale, labels and uncertainty where relevant.
5. Check for misleading truncation, aggregation or colour dependence.
6. Ask a reader to describe the finding and its limits.

**Guided practice**
Create two charts from a small fictional dataset: one that communicates category comparison and one that communicates change over time. Then intentionally inspect a flawed chart and list at least four ways it could mislead.

**Independent evidence task**
Submit raw fictional data, chart selection rationale, two finished charts, a critique of one misleading display and a five-question reader test. Do not use private financial, health or school records.

**Assessment rubric**
1. chart/data fit
2. accuracy of scales and denominators
3. labelling and accessibility
4. interpretation limits
5. reader-test results and revision
**Scoring:** score each dimension 0–3 with a written rationale (maximum 15). A typical developing-level target is 11/15, but safety-critical units require all safety conditions to be met. A score is evidence about this task, not automatic mastery.

**Common misconceptions**
- A beautiful chart can still be misleading.
- Averages do not show the full distribution.
- Colour alone should not carry essential meaning.
- Correlation in a dashboard is not proof of causation.

**Transfer links**
Connects FIN statements, BIZ metrics, CYBER monitoring and PSY risk interpretation. Honest data display is a cross-disciplinary competency.

**Safety, ethics and scope**
Use synthetic or public non-sensitive datasets. Label simulated data clearly and do not imply fictional numbers are real measurements.

**Retention and reassessment**
Recreate one chart from a new dataset without looking at the original and explain why the same chart type remains or changes.

## ART-ADV-001 — Perspective, light and form: constructing believable space
**Discipline:** ART  |  **Level:** advanced_developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 6–9 hours across several drawing sessions
**Prerequisites:** ART-DEV-001; ART-DEV-002; basic line, shape and value vocabulary

**Learning outcomes**
- construct a simple one- or two-point perspective scene
- identify horizon, vanishing points and converging orthogonals
- describe how light direction affects form, cast shadow and value grouping
- use measurement and negative space to check proportion
- revise a drawing using observed evidence rather than symbol-based guessing

**Core model**
Perspective is a system for representing three-dimensional spatial relationships on a two-dimensional surface. In linear perspective, parallel sets of receding edges may converge toward vanishing points on the horizon; not every edge converges to the same point. Form is communicated through contour, overlap, scale, value and shadow. A simple light model identifies source direction, illuminated plane, terminator, core shadow, reflected light and cast shadow, but real materials and multiple light sources complicate the pattern. These are observational tools, not rigid formulas for every image.

**Worked example**
A box viewed at an angle has vertical edges that remain vertical in a simple one-point setup, while depth edges converge toward a vanishing point. If the cast shadow points in a direction inconsistent with the light source, the object may appear spatially confused.

**Method / procedure**
1. Set a simple scene with one clear light source.
2. Mark eye level/horizon and major object boundaries.
3. Use sighting and negative-space comparisons to place proportions.
4. Construct major perspective edges lightly before details.
5. Group values into light, midtone and shadow families.
6. Check cast-shadow direction and overlap cues.
7. Revise structural errors before rendering texture.

**Guided practice**
Draw a box, cylinder and sphere from observation under one lamp. Make a second drawing of a simple room corner using one-point perspective. Annotate the horizon, vanishing point and light direction.

**Independent evidence task**
Submit three studies, construction lines, a value map, one revised final drawing and a short note identifying the three largest corrections made. Photograph work in even light so evidence remains legible.

**Assessment rubric**
1. spatial construction
2. proportion and negative-space checking
3. light/value consistency
4. quality of observation and revision
5. clear annotation and process evidence
**Scoring:** score each dimension 0–3 with a written rationale (maximum 15). A typical developing-level target is 11/15, but safety-critical units require all safety conditions to be met. A score is evidence about this task, not automatic mastery.

**Common misconceptions**
- Perspective is not simply making distant objects smaller; overlap and convergence also matter.
- Reflected light is usually weaker than directly lit planes in a simple setup.
- Outlines alone do not communicate volume.
- A ruler can assist construction but cannot replace observation.

**Transfer links**
Connects AEST spatial hierarchy and ART composition to engineering diagrams, photography and visual explanation.

**Safety, ethics and scope**
Use ordinary drawing materials in a ventilated, well-lit workspace. Avoid unsafe blades, solvents or aerosols; ask an adult before using unfamiliar materials.

**Retention and reassessment**
Repeat the same construction method with a different object and viewing angle to test whether the spatial rules transfer.

## ART-ADV-002 — Visual research, reference ethics and iterative concept development
**Discipline:** ART  |  **Level:** advanced_developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 5–8 hours
**Prerequisites:** ART-DEV-002; basic source citation and file organisation; awareness of copyright and consent

**Learning outcomes**
- turn a creative brief into visual research questions
- collect references with provenance and usage constraints
- separate observation, influence and direct copying
- generate multiple distinct concepts before selecting one
- document iteration and explain why design decisions changed

**Core model**
Visual research is a process of gathering evidence about subject, context, materials, audience and visual language. A reference can inform anatomy, lighting, architecture, colour or composition, but it does not automatically grant permission to reproduce or publish it. Keep source, creator, URL, access date and licence/permission notes. Distinguish studies made for private learning from public or commercial use. Concept development benefits from divergent exploration (several genuinely different directions) followed by convergent selection against the brief. Iteration should test a question, not merely decorate the first idea.

**Worked example**
A brief asks for an original poster about urban trees. Research may include local tree forms, public-space signage and poster layouts. The learner creates three concepts: a typographic message, a data-led canopy comparison and a character-led narrative. The final direction is chosen for audience clarity and evidence, not because it resembles one reference most closely.

**Method / procedure**
1. Rewrite the brief as audience, purpose, constraints and success criteria.
2. List research questions before searching.
3. Collect a small, relevant set of references and record provenance/rights.
4. Annotate what each reference teaches; do not trace or copy it as a finished solution.
5. Generate at least three visibly different concepts.
6. Compare concepts against the brief and user needs.
7. Prototype, obtain feedback and log revisions.

**Guided practice**
Choose a fictional campaign brief. Build a six-reference research board with source notes, then create three thumbnail directions that differ in concept, not just colour. Explain which evidence informed each.

**Independent evidence task**
Submit the brief analysis, annotated reference board, rights log, three concept sheets, selection matrix, one prototype, feedback and revision log. Clearly mark any AI-generated or synthetic reference material and never present it as documentary evidence.

**Assessment rubric**
1. quality and relevance of research questions
2. reference provenance and rights awareness
3. conceptual diversity
4. selection rationale against brief
5. traceable iteration and originality
**Scoring:** score each dimension 0–3 with a written rationale (maximum 15). A typical developing-level target is 11/15, but safety-critical units require all safety conditions to be met. A score is evidence about this task, not automatic mastery.

**Common misconceptions**
- A reference board is not a permission slip.
- Changing colour or cropping does not automatically make copied work original.
- More references do not guarantee better research.
- Iteration should answer a design question rather than add random polish.

**Transfer links**
Connects AEST critique, PERF production planning, BIZ audience research and RES source provenance. The research log is useful in any project.

**Safety, ethics and scope**
Do not use private images of people without permission. Respect licences, attribution and platform terms. Avoid reproducing hateful, humiliating or exploitative imagery.

**Retention and reassessment**
Repeat the concept-generation process for a new brief and compare whether the ideas are meaningfully distinct and traceable to the research.

## INDEP-ADV-001 — Personal administration: records, budgets, purchases and consumer judgement
**Discipline:** INDEP  |  **Level:** advanced_developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 4–6 hours
**Prerequisites:** INDEP-DEV-001; INDEP-DEV-002; basic arithmetic and digital file organisation

**Learning outcomes**
- organise a simple personal task and document system
- compare total cost, recurring cost, warranty and practical need in a purchase
- recognise common misleading sales tactics and urgency cues
- maintain a privacy-conscious record of decisions and receipts
- identify which decisions require parent/guardian involvement

**Core model**
Personal administration reduces avoidable errors by making obligations, records and decisions visible. A simple system may include a calendar, task list, secure folder for non-sensitive receipts and a purchase comparison sheet. Total cost includes more than the displayed price: delivery, recurring fees, required accessories, repairability and replacement risk may matter. Consumer judgement separates need, budget, evidence, alternatives and pressure tactics. A young person should involve a parent/guardian for accounts, contracts, subscriptions, payments, personal data and consequential purchases.

**Worked example**
Two devices have different sticker prices. Option A costs less upfront but requires a subscription and proprietary accessories; Option B costs more but has no recurring fee and a longer warranty. A fair comparison lists expected costs over a stated period and identifies uncertain assumptions.

**Method / procedure**
1. State the need and the maximum amount available with guardian agreement.
2. List essential features separately from preferences.
3. Compare at least three credible options, including not buying.
4. Calculate upfront and recurring costs over a defined period.
5. Check seller identity, return policy, warranty and independent evidence.
6. Record the decision and avoid storing passwords or sensitive identifiers in the project log.

**Guided practice**
Use a fictional purchase scenario with three options, delivery costs and subscription terms. Build a comparison table and explain which unknowns could change the decision.

**Independent evidence task**
Create a personal-administration template using fictional data: weekly task view, purchase comparison, receipt naming convention and privacy checklist. Do not upload real account statements, addresses, phone numbers or payment details.

**Assessment rubric**
1. completeness of cost comparison
2. evidence and seller-risk checks
3. recognition of pressure tactics
4. privacy and record organisation
5. appropriate guardian escalation
**Scoring:** score each dimension 0–3 with a written rationale (maximum 15). A typical developing-level target is 11/15, but safety-critical units require all safety conditions to be met. A score is evidence about this task, not automatic mastery.

**Common misconceptions**
- The lowest sticker price is not always the lowest total cost.
- A countdown timer does not prove a genuine deadline.
- A warranty and a return policy are different.
- A spreadsheet should not contain passwords or full payment credentials.

**Transfer links**
Connects FIN cash-flow literacy, CYBER data protection and WORK systems. The same comparison framework supports school supplies, devices and subscriptions.

**Safety, ethics and scope**
Do not create financial accounts, sign contracts, share identity documents, make online payments or contact unknown sellers independently. Involve a parent/guardian. Keep all practice data fictional.

**Retention and reassessment**
Revisit the template after a month using a new fictional scenario and check whether recurring costs and privacy risks are still visible.

## INDEP-ADV-002 — Household systems: maintenance checks, hazard awareness and escalation
**Discipline:** INDEP  |  **Level:** advanced_developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 3–5 hours supervised observation and documentation
**Prerequisites:** INDEP-DEV-001; INDEP-DEV-002; ability to follow written safety instructions

**Learning outcomes**
- distinguish routine observation from repair or intervention
- identify common household hazard categories and safe boundaries
- create a simple inspection and escalation checklist
- communicate a hazard clearly with location, observation and urgency
- understand why some tasks require trained adults or professionals

**Core model**
Household independence includes noticing, documenting and escalating—not attempting every repair. A safe system separates observe, isolate if safe, warn, report and wait for qualified help. Hazards may involve electricity, gas, fire, water, chemicals, structural damage, sharp tools or blocked exits. The correct response depends on the hazard and local emergency guidance. Never touch exposed wiring, smell for gas, mix chemicals, climb to inspect roofs or attempt appliance repairs. The learner’s task is to recognise boundaries and communicate accurately.

**Worked example**
A learner notices water near an electrical appliance. They do not touch the appliance or step into the water to unplug it. They warn others, move away, tell a responsible adult immediately and follow that adult’s safe response. The observation record states what was seen, where and when without guessing the cause.

**Method / procedure**
1. Observe from a safe distance; do not test a suspected hazard.
2. Identify the category without diagnosing its source.
3. Keep people away if this can be done without approaching danger.
4. Tell a responsible adult promptly; use local emergency services for immediate danger.
5. Record only factual observations and time.
6. Wait for qualified personnel; do not improvise repairs.

**Guided practice**
With a parent/guardian, walk through a safe household checklist: clear exits, visible smoke-alarm status (adult checks device operation), safe storage labels, obvious trip hazards and emergency contact location. Learner observes and records; adult handles all testing or intervention.

**Independent evidence task**
Submit a household observation checklist approved by a guardian, three sample hazard reports using fictional examples and a reflection on tasks deliberately not attempted. No photographs of security systems, keys, access codes or private household layouts.

**Assessment rubric**
1. hazard recognition without overclaiming
2. safe boundaries and escalation
3. clarity of factual report
4. appropriate adult/professional involvement
5. privacy and checklist quality
**Scoring:** score each dimension 0–3 with a written rationale (maximum 15). A typical developing-level target is 11/15, but safety-critical units require all safety conditions to be met. A score is evidence about this task, not automatic mastery.

**Common misconceptions**
- Independence does not mean repairing everything alone.
- A familiar appliance can still be dangerous when damaged.
- Smell, sound or appearance alone may not reveal the full hazard.
- A checklist is not a substitute for local emergency instructions.

**Transfer links**
Connects CYBER incident reporting, WORK checklists and LEAD escalation. The observe–report–handoff pattern is broadly useful.

**Safety, ethics and scope**
Adult-supervised observation only. Never handle electrical, gas, chemical, fire, structural or medical hazards. For immediate danger, move away and alert adults/emergency services according to local guidance.

**Retention and reassessment**
Review the checklist with a guardian periodically; update it only with adult approval and never record sensitive security details.

## Cross-unit assessment rules
