# Wave 3 — Six-Discipline Teaching Pack, Batch 01
**Production message:** 12 of 20  |  **Status:** authored working draft
**Disciplines:** BODY, ATH, PERF, AEST, ART, INDEP
**Scope:** 12 developing-level instructional units; two per discipline. These units extend the entry-level baselines and do not complete any discipline.

## How to use this pack
ATHENA assigns the objective and evidence contract. The learner selects reputable external sources, studies independently, then returns with a closed-book explanation, an application artifact and source provenance. An evaluator scores the evidence and proposes a structured update. The learner previews and approves any persistent commit. Reading this pack alone does not grant mastery.

## Shared evidence contract
For every unit, retain: (1) objective and unit ID; (2) source title, author/organisation, URL or bibliographic locator, access date and relevant section; (3) learner explanation produced without copying; (4) application artifact or supervised demonstration; (5) rubric scores with evaluator rationale; (6) uncertainty, limitations and safety notes; (7) learner confidence; (8) proposed status transition. No automatic mastery or permanent completion.

## BODY-DEV-001 — Human systems, homeostasis and feedback
**Discipline:** BODY  |  **Level:** developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 3–4 hours
**Prerequisites:** BODY-FOUND-001; basic cell and tissue vocabulary; ability to interpret a simple labelled diagram

**Learning outcomes**
- trace a basic stimulus–response loop
- explain how respiratory and circulatory systems cooperate during activity
- distinguish a short-term response from a longer-term adaptation
- state limits of a simplified model

**Core model**
The body is an interacting system, not a collection of independent parts. A useful first model is input or disturbance → sensor/receptor → control centre → effector → response → feedback. Negative feedback tends to reduce a deviation from a regulated range; it does not mean that the response is bad. The nervous and endocrine systems coordinate many responses, while organ systems exchange matter, energy and information.

**Worked example**
During exercise, working muscles use more oxygen and release more carbon dioxide and heat. Receptors and control systems contribute to increased breathing and heart rate. Blood transports gases and heat; lungs exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide; muscles use oxygen in cellular respiration. The response supports the activity but does not imply that every measured value is constant or that one organ acts alone.

**Procedure / method**
1. Name the variable or disturbance. 2. Identify the receptor or sensing process only at the level supported by the source. 3. Identify the coordinating system. 4. Name the effector and response. 5. Explain how the response changes the original disturbance. 6. Mark where the model omits detail.

**Guided practice**
Draw a five-box feedback loop for body temperature during a warm day. Add a second diagram showing how breathing and circulation support a short bout of running. Use arrows and label what is transported.

**Independent evidence task**
Create a one-page systems map for one everyday physiological challenge (exercise, heat, or digestion). Explain three links between systems, include one feedback loop, and list two simplifications or unknowns. Do not diagnose yourself or infer a medical condition from a wearable reading.

**Assessment rubric**
0–3 each: systems accuracy; causal sequence; connection across systems; clarity of diagram; limits/uncertainty. Target ≥11/15 with no unsafe medical claim.

**Common misconceptions**
Homeostasis does not mean every variable is perfectly fixed; negative feedback is not a moral judgement; heart rate alone does not measure total fitness; diagrams simplify parallel and overlapping control pathways.

**Transfer links**
Connects to ATH load management, PERF breath coordination and scientific systems modelling. Transfer the feedback-loop method to a thermostat or irrigation controller, while noting biological systems are more complex.

**Safety and ethical boundary**
Educational anatomy only. Persistent symptoms, pain, breathing difficulty or health concerns belong with a trusted adult and qualified health professional; no self-diagnosis.

**Retention suggestion**
Use a short closed-book retrieval after the first study session, a fresh application after several days, and a later transfer task after a longer gap. Set review timing from observed performance and item type; do not impose a universal interval. If performance fails, diagnose the specific gap and relearn without erasing prior evidence.

## BODY-DEV-002 — Nutrition, sleep, recovery and health-claim literacy
**Discipline:** BODY  |  **Level:** developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 3–4 hours
**Prerequisites:** BODY-FOUND-001; basic fractions and percentages; ability to compare claims with evidence

**Learning outcomes**
- describe broad roles of carbohydrates, fats, proteins, vitamins, minerals and water
- explain why energy and nutrient needs vary
- distinguish sleep opportunity, sleep quality and recovery
- evaluate a health claim for evidence, scope and uncertainty

**Core model**
Nutrition and recovery are multi-factor systems. Macronutrients supply energy and structural functions; micronutrients support processes but are not interchangeable with calories; water supports transport and temperature regulation. Sleep supports many brain and body functions. Recovery is influenced by training load, sleep, food access, stress, illness and individual variation. A single food, supplement, metric or routine cannot guarantee health or performance.

**Worked example**
A headline says a drink “boosts stamina by 40%.” Before accepting it, ask: 40% of what measure? Compared with what? How many participants? Were they similar to the intended audience? Was the study independent and replicated? Does the result apply to normal activity or only a narrow laboratory test?

**Procedure / method**
1. Identify the exact claim and outcome. 2. Separate mechanism speculation from measured result. 3. Inspect who was studied, sample size, comparison and duration. 4. Look for absolute values, uncertainty and adverse effects. 5. Check whether independent evidence agrees. 6. Decide what conclusion is justified—and what is not.

**Guided practice**
Compare three fictional claims: “water is necessary for normal function”; “this snack cures fatigue”; “one sleep score predicts athletic potential.” Classify each as broadly supported, unsupported/overstated or requiring context, and explain why.

**Independent evidence task**
Build a neutral evidence brief on one basic nutrition or sleep topic using at least two reputable educational/health sources. Record author/organisation, date, exact claim, evidence type, limitations and a cautious conclusion. Do not create a restrictive diet or supplement plan.

**Assessment rubric**
0–3 each: nutrient/recovery concepts; evidence appraisal; distinction between association and causation; source provenance; appropriately cautious conclusion. Target ≥12/15.

**Common misconceptions**
More protein is not automatically better; supplements are not a substitute for food/sleep; one night or wearable score cannot diagnose recovery; correlation does not prove causation.

**Transfer links**
Supports ATH session planning and PERF vocal recovery. Apply the claim-checking method to marketing, social media and product reviews.

**Safety and ethical boundary**
No calorie targets, weight-loss prescriptions, supplement recommendations or diagnosis. For a young learner, nutrition and sleep changes should be discussed with a parent/guardian and qualified clinician when health concerns exist.

**Retention suggestion**
Use a short closed-book retrieval after the first study session, a fresh application after several days, and a later transfer task after a longer gap. Set review timing from observed performance and item type; do not impose a universal interval. If performance fails, diagnose the specific gap and relearn without erasing prior evidence.

## ATH-DEV-001 — Acceleration, deceleration, landing and change of direction
**Discipline:** ATH  |  **Level:** developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 3–5 coached practice sessions
**Prerequisites:** ATH-FOUND-001; basic balance and body awareness; safe open practice space

**Learning outcomes**
- describe acceleration and braking as distinct movement tasks
- demonstrate controlled low-intensity landing mechanics
- identify space, traction and fatigue constraints
- design a progressive practice with recovery and stop rules

**Core model**
Speed is not only producing force; it also includes applying and absorbing force in the correct direction at the correct time. Acceleration requires a forward-directed impulse. Deceleration requires reducing momentum under control. Change of direction combines braking, repositioning and re-acceleration. Technique depends on task, surface, footwear, strength, coordination and fatigue; one “perfect” cue does not fit every athlete.

**Worked example**
A player sprinting toward a cone cannot instantly turn at full speed. They must reduce speed before or during the turn, place the body to manage force, then push into the new direction. Approaching too fast may increase slipping or loss of control.

**Procedure / method**
1. Inspect the surface and clear hazards. 2. Warm up progressively. 3. Rehearse marching, low-speed starts and controlled stops. 4. Add a wide-angle turn before sharper turns. 5. Increase speed only when control is repeatable. 6. Rest enough to preserve movement quality. 7. Stop for pain, dizziness, unusual breathlessness or unsafe conditions.

**Guided practice**
On a safe, dry, non-slip surface, rehearse five gentle accelerations over a short distance and controlled stops. Then perform low-speed wide turns in both directions. Record perceived control and any discomfort; do not chase a maximum time.

**Independent evidence task**
Create a 2-week practice log with two short sessions per week. Include warm-up, low-to-moderate drills, rest, surface check, technique cue, and progression criterion. Demonstrate one controlled stop and one direction change on video only if safe and consented.

**Assessment rubric**
0–3 each: safety setup; movement control; progressive loading; observation quality; recovery/reflection. Target ≥12/15; unsafe setup or pushing through pain is an automatic fail.

**Common misconceptions**
Agility is not simply running around cones quickly; fatigue can degrade technique; maximal effort is not required to learn movement; one cue cannot diagnose an athlete’s mechanics.

**Transfer links**
Connects to BODY biomechanics and football tactical movement. Use the same progression logic for dance, court sports and everyday stair or obstacle navigation.

**Safety and ethical boundary**
For a developing athlete, prioritize supervision, safe surfaces, modest volume and technique. No maximal sprint, heavy loading or plyometric testing without qualified coaching and age-appropriate preparation.

**Retention suggestion**
Use a short closed-book retrieval after the first study session, a fresh application after several days, and a later transfer task after a longer gap. Set review timing from observed performance and item type; do not impose a universal interval. If performance fails, diagnose the specific gap and relearn without erasing prior evidence.

## ATH-DEV-002 — Football scanning, support angles and decision-making
**Discipline:** ATH  |  **Level:** developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 4–6 practice sessions
**Prerequisites:** ATH-FOUND-001; basic passing and receiving; understanding of teammate/opponent roles

**Learning outcomes**
- scan before receiving and identify relevant information
- create a useful support angle rather than hiding behind a defender
- select a simple action based on pressure, space and teammate position
- review decisions using evidence rather than outcome alone

**Core model**
Tactical skill is perception–action coupling: observe cues, interpret the situation, choose an action and execute it. A successful decision is not defined only by whether the pass happened to work. Evaluate the information available at the time, the risk, the team objective and the execution. Support angles create more than one passing lane and help the ball carrier retain options.

**Worked example**
A teammate receives with an opponent approaching from the front. A supporting player checks a shoulder, moves diagonally away from the defender’s cover shadow and offers a clear passing lane. If the defender blocks the route, the supporting player adjusts rather than demanding the ball in the same space.

**Procedure / method**
1. Before the ball arrives, scan ball, nearest pressure, space and teammates. 2. Open the body where possible to see more than one option. 3. Move to create an angle and distance that can be reached with a pass. 4. Communicate briefly. 5. After the action, recover into a useful team position. 6. Review the decision separately from technical execution.

**Guided practice**
In a small group, use a simple 3v1 or 4v2 keep-away game in a safe space. The supporting player must scan before receiving and move after passing. Reduce pressure or enlarge the area if players become crowded or technique collapses.

**Independent evidence task**
Review five short sequences from a training game or a consented recording. For each, record: what was visible before receiving, available options, chosen action, team consequence and one alternative. Do not judge solely by pass completion.

**Assessment rubric**
0–3 each: scanning evidence; support-angle quality; contextual decision; communication; reflective accuracy. Target ≥11/15.

**Common misconceptions**
“Always pass forward” is not a universal rule; a completed pass can still be a poor decision; standing near the ball is not automatically useful support; hindsight bias distorts review.

**Transfer links**
Links to PSY attention and decision-making, LEAD communication and video analysis. Use cue–interpret–act–review in any fast team task.

**Safety and ethical boundary**
Keep drills age-appropriate, avoid collisions and unsafe surfaces, and do not use public footage of others without permission.

**Retention suggestion**
Use a short closed-book retrieval after the first study session, a fresh application after several days, and a later transfer task after a longer gap. Set review timing from observed performance and item type; do not impose a universal interval. If performance fails, diagnose the specific gap and relearn without erasing prior evidence.

## PERF-DEV-001 — Safe vocal production, breath coordination and fatigue awareness
**Discipline:** PERF  |  **Level:** developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 2–3 weeks of short practice
**Prerequisites:** PERF-FOUND-001; ability to follow a short warm-up; basic awareness of comfortable speaking/singing range

**Learning outcomes**
- describe breath coordination without forcing airflow
- use gentle warm-up and cool-down habits
- distinguish expressive effort from strain
- recognise signs to stop and seek appropriate help

**Core model**
Voice production involves airflow, vocal-fold vibration, resonance and articulation. Efficient coordination is not the same as maximum volume or maximum breath pressure. Practice should begin comfortably, use short repetitions and stop before fatigue accumulates. Hydration and general health matter, but no single trick guarantees vocal health. Pain, persistent hoarseness or voice loss is not a technique challenge to push through.

**Worked example**
A learner tries to sing a high note louder by pushing more air and tightening the neck. The sound may become strained. A safer experiment is to reduce volume, return to a comfortable pitch, release unnecessary tension and seek qualified instruction if discomfort persists.

**Procedure / method**
1. Begin with relaxed posture and easy breathing. 2. Use gentle humming or comfortable lip trills if they feel easy. 3. Keep pitch and volume within a comfortable range. 4. Rest between short sets. 5. Notice effort, not just sound. 6. Stop for pain, persistent roughness or loss of voice. 7. Record practice duration and response, not a forced range maximum.

**Guided practice**
Complete a 3–5 minute gentle warm-up using comfortable humming and simple speech rhythm. Record one short phrase at a comfortable volume. Listen for clarity and ease, not loudness or extreme pitch.

**Independent evidence task**
Maintain a 7-day voice-practice log with short, comfortable sessions. Include warm-up, duration, task, perceived effort and any fatigue. Submit a short reflection on which conditions supported ease and when you rested.

**Assessment rubric**
0–3 each: safe warm-up; comfortable coordination; fatigue awareness; practice consistency; reflective evidence. Target ≥12/15; pushing through pain or persistent hoarseness is a critical fail.

**Common misconceptions**
More air does not always mean better tone; loudness is not projection quality; range is not a competition; pain is not proof of progress.

**Transfer links**
Connects to BODY respiration/recovery and COMM speaking. Apply gradual practice and fatigue monitoring to any repeated motor skill.

**Safety and ethical boundary**
No forced high notes, extreme vocal exercises, shouting challenges or breath-holding contests. Persistent hoarseness, pain or voice loss should be discussed with a trusted adult and qualified clinician/voice professional.

**Retention suggestion**
Use a short closed-book retrieval after the first study session, a fresh application after several days, and a later transfer task after a longer gap. Set review timing from observed performance and item type; do not impose a universal interval. If performance fails, diagnose the specific gap and relearn without erasing prior evidence.

## PERF-DEV-002 — Interpretation, stage presence and deliberate rehearsal
**Discipline:** PERF  |  **Level:** developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 3–5 rehearsal sessions
**Prerequisites:** PERF-FOUND-001; PERF-DEV-001; ability to prepare a short spoken or musical piece

**Learning outcomes**
- identify the intention and audience of a short performance
- shape phrasing, pacing and emphasis to communicate meaning
- rehearse one variable at a time
- use playback or feedback without treating taste as objective fact

**Core model**
Performance is communication under constraints. Interpretation begins with the work’s meaning, context and intended effect. Stage presence is not simply high energy; it is purposeful attention, readable choices and connection with an audience. Deliberate rehearsal isolates a target (timing, articulation, phrasing, gesture), tests a change and records whether it improves the intended effect.

**Worked example**
A 45-second story has an important reveal. If the performer speaks every sentence at the same speed and emphasis, the reveal may be unclear. A deliberate pause, changed pace and precise emphasis can guide attention—provided the choices fit the text and audience.

**Procedure / method**
1. Identify the audience and purpose. 2. Mark meaning units and key words. 3. Choose one expressive variable to test. 4. Rehearse a short section. 5. Record or ask for specific feedback. 6. Compare against the intended effect. 7. Keep, revise or discard the change and rehearse the whole piece.

**Guided practice**
Prepare a 30–60 second passage. Perform it once neutrally, then make one deliberate change to pacing or emphasis. Ask a listener what meaning they noticed before explaining your intention.

**Independent evidence task**
Create a rehearsal dossier: text or piece selection, intended audience, interpretation notes, three rehearsal iterations, feedback, final performance and a reflection distinguishing your intention from audience response.

**Assessment rubric**
0–3 each: interpretation; expressive control; rehearsal method; audience awareness; use of feedback. Target ≥11/15.

**Common misconceptions**
Stage presence is not extroversion; louder is not automatically clearer; one listener’s taste is not universal; recording can reveal timing but cannot fully reproduce a live audience.

**Transfer links**
Supports ENG/COMM rhetoric, AEST hierarchy and ART visual storytelling. The same iteration loop works for presentations, demonstrations and interviews.

**Safety and ethical boundary**
Respect copyright and performance permissions. Do not pressure a learner to perform publicly or disclose personal material.

**Retention suggestion**
Use a short closed-book retrieval after the first study session, a fresh application after several days, and a later transfer task after a longer gap. Set review timing from observed performance and item type; do not impose a universal interval. If performance fails, diagnose the specific gap and relearn without erasing prior evidence.

## AEST-DEV-001 — Visual hierarchy, grid systems and information layout
**Discipline:** AEST  |  **Level:** developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 3–5 hours
**Prerequisites:** AEST-FOUND-001; basic shape and text editing; ability to identify a target audience and task

**Learning outcomes**
- identify the primary task and information priority
- use size, position, spacing and contrast to establish hierarchy
- apply a simple alignment/grid system
- evaluate a layout for readability at realistic viewing size

**Core model**
Visual hierarchy directs attention. It is created through relative scale, position, contrast, whitespace, grouping, alignment and typography. A grid provides repeatable structure; it does not require every element to be identical. Layout quality is judged against a communication task and audience, not decoration alone. A design that looks attractive at full zoom may fail on a small phone screen.

**Worked example**
A poster has a title, date, venue and registration instruction. If the decorative background dominates and the date is tiny, the viewer may enjoy the image but fail to act. Enlarging the event title, grouping date and venue, and giving the action a clear position improves task completion.

**Procedure / method**
1. Write the audience and single primary action. 2. Rank content as primary, secondary or supporting. 3. Sketch three thumbnail layouts. 4. Choose a grid and consistent margins. 5. Set type scale and spacing. 6. Check contrast and reading order. 7. Test at actual display size and revise.

**Guided practice**
Redesign a fictional event card containing title, date, location and call to action. Make two variants: phone-first and A4 print. Keep content identical and explain the hierarchy choices.

**Independent evidence task**
Produce a one-page layout case study with three thumbnails, one final design, grid/margin notes, type scale, contrast check and a short test from two viewers. Record where they looked first and whether they found the key details.

**Assessment rubric**
0–3 each: task clarity; hierarchy; alignment/spacing; legibility/accessibility; evidence-led iteration. Target ≥11/15.

**Common misconceptions**
More decoration does not equal stronger design; a grid is a guide not a cage; contrast is not only colour; personal preference does not replace audience testing.

**Transfer links**
Links to ART composition, CYBER interface security cues and WORK documentation. Hierarchy also applies to dashboards, reports and instructions.

**Safety and ethical boundary**
Use licensed or self-created assets; do not imitate a living designer’s exact signature style or use protected logos without permission.

**Retention suggestion**
Use a short closed-book retrieval after the first study session, a fresh application after several days, and a later transfer task after a longer gap. ## AEST-DEV-002 — Colour relationships, contrast and accessible visual communication
**Discipline:** AEST  |  **Level:** developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 3–4 hours
**Prerequisites:** AEST-DEV-001; basic colour naming and visual comparison

**Learning outcomes**
- distinguish hue, value and saturation
- explain how foreground/background contrast affects legibility
- build a restrained palette with functional roles
- avoid communicating essential information through colour alone

**Core model**
Colour has several dimensions: hue (colour family), value/lightness and saturation/intensity. Relationships depend on context and neighbouring colours. Contrast must be evaluated for the actual text, size, weight and background. Colour can communicate categories or mood, but meaning varies by culture and context. Accessibility requires more than choosing a fashionable palette: labels, shape, position and text should carry meaning too.

**Worked example**
A chart uses red for “late” and green for “on time.” Some viewers may not distinguish the categories reliably, and a grayscale print may erase the difference. Adding direct labels and distinct symbols preserves meaning even when colour perception or display changes.

**Procedure / method**
1. Define the communication purpose. 2. Choose a small base palette. 3. Assign roles (background, text, accent, status). 4. Check text/background contrast at final size. 5. Test in grayscale and at low brightness. 6. Add non-colour cues. 7. Record palette values and revise after testing.

**Guided practice**
Create a three-category status panel using colour plus labels/icons. Test it in grayscale and at reduced screen brightness. Ask a peer to identify each state without being told the colour key.

**Independent evidence task**
Submit a palette sheet with hue/value/saturation notes, functional role tokens, contrast measurements from a named checker/tool, grayscale test and a brief limitation statement. Do not claim full accessibility from a single contrast number.

**Assessment rubric**
0–3 each: colour model; functional palette; contrast/legibility; redundant encoding; testing and limitations. Target ≥12/15.

**Common misconceptions**
Complementary colours are not always readable together; a contrast ratio alone does not guarantee good design; colour meanings are not universal; dark mode is not automatically more accessible.

**Transfer links**
Supports photography, UI design, data visualisation and ART painting. Use redundant visual cues in safety labels and technical diagrams.

**Safety and ethical boundary**
Avoid using colour alone for warnings or critical information. Verify accessibility guidance against current standards when publishing production interfaces.

**Retention suggestion**
Use a short closed-book retrieval after the first study session, a fresh application after several days, and a later transfer task after a longer gap. Set review timing from observed performance and item type; do not impose a universal interval. If performance fails, diagnose the specific gap and relearn without erasing prior evidence.

## ART-DEV-001 — Observational drawing: proportion, form and value
**Discipline:** ART  |  **Level:** developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 5–7 short practice sessions
**Prerequisites:** ART-FOUND-001; basic line and shape drawing; simple household object as reference

**Learning outcomes**
- measure relative proportions using visual comparison
- construct an object from simple 2D/3D forms
- identify light direction and major value groups
- revise a drawing using observation rather than symbol memory

**Core model**
Observational drawing is a process of comparing relationships, not drawing what an object is “supposed” to look like. Proportion compares relative lengths, angles and positions. Construction simplifies complex objects into basic forms. Value describes lightness/darkness and helps communicate volume. Edges vary: some are sharp, some soft and some lost.

**Worked example**
When drawing a mug, a learner may draw a perfect circle for the opening because that is the symbol they remember. From an angled view, the opening appears as an ellipse. Comparing its width to height and aligning the handle to the body produces a more faithful drawing.

**Procedure / method**
1. Choose a stable object and fixed viewpoint. 2. Mark the overall bounding box. 3. Compare width/height and major angles. 4. Block in large shapes before details. 5. Identify light direction and three broad value groups. 6. Check negative spaces and alignment. 7. Correct the largest mismatch first.

**Guided practice**
Draw a simple cup or box in three passes: 2-minute gesture, 10-minute construction and 15-minute value study. Keep the object and viewpoint unchanged. Compare the three versions and identify the biggest improvement.

**Independent evidence task**
Create a five-study sheet of one object from the same view: silhouette, construction, proportion check, value block-in and final drawing. Annotate two corrections made from direct observation.

**Assessment rubric**
0–3 each: proportion observation; form construction; value grouping; iterative correction; annotation/reflection. Target ≥11/15.

**Common misconceptions**
Details cannot rescue incorrect large proportions; shading is not random smudging; an ellipse changes with viewpoint; “style” is not a substitute for observational evidence.

**Transfer links**
Supports AEST visual hierarchy, photography composition and ART perspective. Measurement-by-comparison also helps diagramming and technical sketching.

**Safety and ethical boundary**
Use safe materials and a comfortable posture; avoid prolonged strain. Use references lawfully and credit sources when required.

**Retention suggestion**
Use a short closed-book retrieval after the first study session, a fresh application after several days, and a later transfer task after a longer gap. Set review timing from observed performance and item type; do not impose a universal interval. If performance fails, diagnose the specific gap and relearn without erasing prior evidence.

## ART-DEV-002 — Composition, visual storytelling and thumbnail iteration
**Discipline:** ART  |  **Level:** developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 4–6 hours
**Prerequisites:** ART-DEV-001; basic understanding of foreground/middle/background

**Learning outcomes**
- define a visual focal point and supporting hierarchy
- use value, shape, scale and direction to guide attention
- generate multiple thumbnails before committing to detail
- explain how composition changes narrative interpretation

**Core model**
Composition is the organisation of visual elements within a frame. The focal point is where attention is intended to land first; secondary elements support or complicate it. Shape, value grouping, scale, overlap, direction and negative space can guide the eye. A story image also implies time, cause, mood or conflict. Thumbnailing is a low-cost way to test alternatives before detailed execution.

**Worked example**
A character stands at a doorway. If the character is small and the bright doorway dominates, the scene may feel like a threshold or unknown destination. If the character fills the frame and the doorway is compressed, the scene may feel tense or trapped. Same subject, different compositional meaning.

**Procedure / method**
1. Write the scene’s central idea in one sentence. 2. Choose a frame ratio. 3. Create at least six small thumbnails. 4. Vary camera distance, focal placement and value grouping. 5. Select two and test silhouettes. 6. Refine one composition. 7. Ask a viewer what they notice and infer before explaining.

**Guided practice**
Make six black-and-white thumbnails for “someone discovers an unexpected message.” Use only large value masses and silhouettes. Pick the two with the clearest narrative and explain the difference.

**Independent evidence task**
Submit a mini visual-development board: premise, six thumbnails, two expanded options, final composition, focal-path annotation and viewer interpretation feedback. Keep the final work original or cite reference material.

**Assessment rubric**
0–3 each: narrative clarity; focal hierarchy; composition variation; iteration quality; reflection and originality. Target ≥11/15.

**Common misconceptions**
The rule of thirds is not a universal law; more detail does not create more story; a focal point need not be centred; a viewer’s interpretation is evidence, not a verdict.

**Transfer links**
Links to AEST layout, PERF staging and ENG narrative structure. The thumbnail method also helps plan slides, videos and product screens.

**Safety and ethical boundary**
Respect copyright and consent when using photographs or people as references; do not publish another person’s likeness without permission.

**Retention suggestion**
Use a short closed-book retrieval after the first study session, a fresh application after several days, and a later transfer task after a longer gap. Set review timing from observed performance and item type; do not impose a universal interval. If performance fails, diagnose the specific gap and relearn without erasing prior evidence.

## INDEP-DEV-001 — Kitchen independence: hygiene, preparation and simple meal workflow
**Discipline:** INDEP  |  **Level:** developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 2–3 supervised practice sessions
**Prerequisites:** INDEP-FOUND-001; ability to read simple instructions; adult supervision and access to a safe kitchen

**Learning outcomes**
- identify clean/dirty workflow and basic contamination risks
- use safe handwashing and surface practices
- follow a simple preparation sequence
- identify tasks requiring adult supervision or specialist equipment

**Core model**
Kitchen work is a controlled workflow with hygiene, heat, sharp tools, timing and storage risks. Preventing cross-contamination means separating raw and ready-to-eat foods, washing hands and surfaces, and following reliable storage/cooking guidance. Mise en place—preparing ingredients and tools before cooking—reduces rushed handling. A recipe is a sequence with dependencies, not merely a list of ingredients.

**Worked example**
A learner prepares a simple vegetable-and-egg dish. Before starting, they wash hands, clear the workspace, gather ingredients, use separate clean utensils for ready-to-eat items and raw egg, and ask an adult to handle hot pans or knives if not trained. They clean surfaces after the task and store leftovers promptly using trusted food-safety guidance.

**Procedure / method**
1. Read the full recipe and identify hazards. 2. Ask an adult to supervise heat, knives and unfamiliar equipment. 3. Wash hands and prepare a clean workspace. 4. Gather ingredients and tools. 5. Keep raw and ready-to-eat items separate. 6. Follow safe cooking and storage instructions from a trusted food-safety authority. 7. Clean and record what to improve.

**Guided practice**
With an adult, prepare a no-cook snack or simple assembly meal using pre-washed ingredients. Practise handwashing, clean surfaces, ingredient sequencing and cleanup. Do not use knives or heat unless specifically supervised and trained.

**Independent evidence task**
Create a kitchen workflow card for one simple meal: ingredients, equipment, preparation sequence, hygiene controls, adult-only steps, storage guidance source and cleanup. Demonstrate the workflow under supervision.

**Assessment rubric**
0–3 each: hygiene; sequencing; hazard recognition; safe supervision/escalation; cleanup and reflection. Target ≥12/15; unsafe food handling is a critical fail.

**Common misconceptions**
Rinsing raw poultry is not a safe substitute for proper cooking and can spread contamination; smell alone cannot prove food is safe; cooking time varies with food and equipment; “natural” does not mean safe.

**Transfer links**
Connects to WORK process design, FIN price comparison and BODY nutrition literacy. Apply workflow sequencing to laundry, workshop cleanup and event preparation.

**Safety and ethical boundary**
A minor must have adult supervision for knives, hot surfaces, appliances and unfamiliar equipment. Follow local food-safety authority guidance; never guess cooking temperatures or storage times.

**Retention suggestion**
Use a short closed-book retrieval after the first study session, a fresh application after several days, and a later transfer task after a longer gap. ## INDEP-DEV-002 — Navigation, appointments and personal contingency planning
**Discipline:** INDEP  |  **Level:** developing  |  **Estimated workload:** 3–4 hours plus one supervised practice route
**Prerequisites:** INDEP-FOUND-001; basic map and clock literacy; ability to contact a trusted adult

**Learning outcomes**
- plan a route using reliable information and time buffers
- identify decision points and safe alternatives
- prepare essential appointment information without oversharing
- use a check-in and escalation plan when a journey changes

**Core model**
Practical independence is not doing everything alone; it is planning, noticing uncertainty and knowing when to ask for help. A route plan includes destination, transport steps, estimated time, cost where relevant, landmarks, contingency options and a trusted contact. Appointment preparation includes purpose, documents needed, questions and a plan for unclear instructions. Personal information should be shared only with legitimate recipients.

**Worked example**
A learner plans a familiar daytime journey with a guardian. They check the route and service updates, identify a safe public place if delayed, agree on a check-in time, carry only necessary information and know whom to contact if the route changes. The plan includes a guardian-approved alternative rather than improvising alone.

**Procedure / method**
1. Confirm destination and appointment time. 2. Use current, reputable route/service information. 3. Add realistic buffers. 4. Identify landmarks and safe public alternatives. 5. Agree a check-in and emergency contact with a guardian. 6. Carry only necessary documents. 7. If uncertain or unsafe, stop in a safe public place and contact the trusted adult or local emergency service as appropriate.

**Guided practice**
Plan a familiar route on paper or a map with a guardian. Mark the start, destination, two landmarks, expected duration, one guardian-approved alternative and the agreed check-in method. Do not undertake a new route alone as part of this exercise.

**Independent evidence task**
Create a reusable personal-operations sheet for one supervised appointment or familiar journey. Include purpose, checklist, route, time buffer, contact plan, privacy check and post-task review. Use fictional data in the submitted artifact.

**Assessment rubric**
0–3 each: route/appointment accuracy; contingency quality; privacy; communication/escalation; review. Target ≥12/15.

**Common misconceptions**
Independence is not refusing help; map estimates are not guarantees; public Wi-Fi or messages are not automatically trustworthy; carrying more personal data is not always safer.

**Transfer links**
Links to WORK planning, CYBER privacy and LEAD communication. Use contingency planning for school events, project deadlines and travel with family.

**Safety and ethical boundary**
For a young learner, route practice and appointments require guardian approval and supervision appropriate to local conditions. Never publish live location, home address, identity documents or private contact details.

**Retention suggestion**
Use a short closed-book retrieval after the first study session, a fresh application after several days, and a later transfer task after a longer gap. Set review timing from observed performance and item type; do not impose a universal interval. If performance fails, diagnose the specific gap and relearn without erasing prior evidence.
