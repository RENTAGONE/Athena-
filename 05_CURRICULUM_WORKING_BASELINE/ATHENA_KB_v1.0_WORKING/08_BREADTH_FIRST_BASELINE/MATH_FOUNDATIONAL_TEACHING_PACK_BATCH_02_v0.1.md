# BASE-MATH-005 — Ratios, rates and proportional reasoning

**Suggested workload:** 3–4 focused sessions of 35–50 minutes  
**Entry knowledge:** BASE-MATH-003 and BASE-MATH-004; multiplication, division, fractions and percentages.

**Learning outcomes**
- Interpret part-to-part and part-to-whole ratios.
- Calculate and compare unit rates with units.
- Scale equivalent ratios and solve proportional problems.
- Distinguish direct proportion from relationships with fixed charges or changing rates.


## What this module teaches

A ratio compares quantities multiplicatively. The ratio 2:3 means that for every 2 units of the first quantity, there are 3 units of the second. A ratio can compare parts to parts (2 red beads to 3 blue beads) or a part to a whole (2 red beads out of 5 total). The context must make clear which comparison is intended.

A rate compares quantities with different units, such as taka per kilogram, kilometres per hour or litres per minute. A unit rate expresses the comparison per one unit of the second quantity. If 3 notebooks cost ৳90, the unit price is ৳30 per notebook. Unit rates let us compare offers with different package sizes, provided the goods and conditions are genuinely comparable.

A proportional relationship preserves a constant multiplicative ratio. If 2 kg of rice costs ৳140 at a constant price per kilogram, 4 kg costs ৳280. The scale factor from 2 to 4 is 2, so the price also doubles. Not every relationship is proportional: a taxi fare with a fixed starting charge plus a per-kilometre charge does not pass through zero.

## Worked example 1 — Equivalent ratios

A paint mixture uses 2 parts blue paint for every 3 parts white paint. A batch with 4 parts blue and 6 parts white has the same ratio because both parts were multiplied by 2. A batch with 4 parts blue and 5 parts white is not equivalent; only one side was scaled. If the total mixture must be 25 cups at the 2:3 ratio, there are 5 equal ratio parts. Each part is 25 ÷ 5 = 5 cups, so use 10 cups blue and 15 cups white.

## Worked example 2 — Compare unit prices

A 500 g packet costs ৳90; a 750 g packet costs ৳126. First convert to price per kilogram. The first costs 90 ÷ 0.5 = ৳180/kg. The second costs 126 ÷ 0.75 = ৳168/kg. The second has the lower unit price, assuming the products are otherwise comparable and there are no different fees or quality differences.

## Worked example 3 — Speed as a rate

A cyclist travels 18 km in 1.5 hours at a constant average speed. Speed = distance ÷ time = 18 ÷ 1.5 = 12 km/h. The unit matters: 12 km/h is not 12 km per minute. If the speed is not constant, this is an average rate, not a claim about every instant.

## Guided practice

1. A drink uses syrup and water in a ratio of 1:4. If 3 cups of syrup are used, how much water is needed?
2. A 2 kg bag costs ৳260. Find the price per 500 g.
3. A printer produces 90 pages in 3 minutes at a steady rate. Find pages per minute and the time for 150 pages.
4. A delivery service charges ৳50 plus ৳12 per kilometre. Explain why total cost is not directly proportional to distance.

**Guided answers:** (1) 12 cups water. (2) ৳65 per 500 g. (3) 30 pages/minute; 5 minutes. (4) At zero kilometres the fare is still ৳50, so the relationship has a fixed starting amount and does not scale from zero.

## Independent exercise set

1. Divide 42 students into two groups in the ratio 4:3. How many are in each group?
2. Which is cheaper per 100 g: 350 g for ৳84 or 500 g for ৳115?
3. A map scale is 1 cm : 5 km. What real distance does 7.2 cm represent?
4. A machine fills 24 bottles in 8 minutes. At the same rate, how many bottles can it fill in 25 minutes?
5. Give an example of a relationship that is not directly proportional and explain which feature breaks proportionality.

**Answer guidance:** (1) Seven total ratio parts; 42 ÷ 7 = 6 per part; groups are 24 and 18. (2) First: ৳24 per 100 g; second: ৳23 per 100 g, so the 500 g packet is cheaper per 100 g. (3) 36 km. (4) 3 bottles/minute × 25 = 75 bottles. (5) Examples include taxi fare with a base charge or a phone plan with a fixed fee plus usage; explain the non-zero starting cost or changing rate.

## Common errors and feedback

- **Adding ratio terms instead of scaling them:** identify the common scale factor and apply it to every part.
- **Comparing package totals rather than unit rates:** convert both offers to the same unit before deciding.
- **Assuming every graph through two points is proportional:** test whether the relationship has a constant ratio and passes through the origin when appropriate.
- **Dropping units:** write the unit rate explicitly; “30” alone is ambiguous.

## Assessment

Solve a recipe-scale problem and a unit-price comparison. Show the ratio structure, identify the scale factor, calculate a unit rate and state assumptions about comparability. **Independent standard:** all quantities scale consistently, units remain attached, and the learner can distinguish proportional from fixed-plus-variable relationships.

## Transfer task

Compare two internet or mobile-data packages with different prices, data amounts and validity periods. Calculate cost per gigabyte per day only if that metric fits the actual usage question. State limits such as speed caps, expiry, rollover and fees. Explain why a lower unit price may not be the best choice if much of the allowance expires unused.

## Entry assumptions and next step

Entry: fractions, decimals, multiplication, division and percentages. Next: algebraic modelling, measurement and rates in physics. Compound ratios and inverse proportion are later extensions.

# BASE-MATH-006 — Negative numbers, signed quantities and number lines

**Suggested workload:** 2–3 focused sessions of 35–50 minutes  
**Entry knowledge:** Whole-number operations, number-line ordering and basic multiplication/division.

**Learning outcomes**
- Interpret signed numbers relative to an explicit reference point.
- Order signed numbers and distinguish absolute value from signed value.
- Add, subtract, multiply and divide signed integers.
- Explain sign rules and verify results using number-line or inverse-operation reasoning.


## What this module teaches

Negative numbers extend the number system to describe values below a chosen reference point or movement in the opposite direction. Temperature below zero, debt relative to a zero balance and elevation below sea level are examples. Zero depends on the reference: zero degrees Celsius is not an absence of temperature, and a zero bank balance is not an absence of money in the world.

On a horizontal number line, values increase to the right and decrease to the left. The opposite of a number is the same distance from zero on the other side: the opposite of 7 is −7. Absolute value describes distance from zero, so |−7| = 7 and |7| = 7. Absolute value is non-negative, but the signed values are not equal.

Addition can be understood as movement. Adding a positive number moves right; adding a negative number moves left. Subtraction can be rewritten as adding the opposite: a − b = a + (−b). This gives a consistent explanation for subtracting negative values. Multiplication and division follow sign patterns, but the meaning should be checked in context; “two negatives make a positive” is not a universal rule for every real-world statement.

## Worked example 1 — Temperature change

The temperature is −3°C and rises by 8°C. Starting at −3 and moving 8 units right gives 5°C. The calculation is −3 + 8 = 5. If it then falls by 7°C, the result is 5 − 7 = −2°C.

## Worked example 2 — Subtracting a negative

Evaluate 4 − (−6). Subtracting −6 means adding its opposite, +6. Therefore 4 − (−6) = 4 + 6 = 10. On a number line, start at 4 and reverse the direction associated with subtracting a negative, moving 6 units right.

## Worked example 3 — Compare signed values

Which is greater, −2 or −5? On the number line, −2 is to the right of −5, so −2 is greater. In a debt context, owing ৳2 is a higher balance than owing ৳5, even though both are below zero.

## Guided practice

1. Start at −4 and move 9 units right. Where do you finish?
2. Evaluate −6 + (−3) and explain the direction of movement.
3. Evaluate 7 − (−2).
4. Order −1, −8, 0 and 3 from least to greatest.

**Guided answers:** (1) 5. (2) −9; move 3 further units left from −6. (3) 9. (4) −8, −1, 0, 3.

## Independent exercise set

1. A diver is 12 m below sea level and rises 7 m. Represent the starting point and movement with signed numbers; find the new position.
2. Evaluate −5 − 8.
3. Evaluate (−4) × 6 and explain the sign using repeated addition.
4. Evaluate (−24) ÷ (−6). Check by multiplication.
5. Explain why |−9| = 9 does not mean −9 = 9.

**Answer guidance:** (1) −12 + 7 = −5 m, still below sea level. (2) −13. (3) −24; six groups of −4 total −24. (4) 4; 4 × (−6) = −24, or (−4) × 6 = −24 as a sign check of division. (5) Absolute value is distance from zero; equality of distances does not imply equality of positions.

## 4. Why the integer sign rules work

For a positive integer, multiplication can be understood as repeated addition: `3 × (−4) = (−4)+(−4)+(−4)=−12`. The negative product follows from distributivity and the fact that a number plus its opposite is zero. Since `0 = (−3) × 0 = (−3) × [4 + (−4)]`, distributivity gives `0 = (−3×4) + (−3×−4)`. The first product is −12, so the second must be +12. Thus a negative times a negative is positive. Division follows because it reverses multiplication when the divisor is non-zero. Division by zero is undefined.

**Additional independent practice:** Evaluate (a) `−7 × 0`, (b) `−8 × −3`, and (c) `36 ÷ −9`. For each, state a multiplication check where possible.

**Answer:** (a) 0; zero times any number is zero. (b) 24, because `−8 × −3 = 24`. (c) −4, because `−4 × −9 = 36`.

## Common errors and feedback

- **Thinking −8 is greater than −3 because 8 is greater than 3:** compare positions on the number line; −8 is farther left.
- **Treating subtraction as always making a value smaller:** subtracting a negative can increase the result.
- **Using “two negatives make a positive” without identifying the operation:** it applies to multiplication/division sign rules and to subtraction of a negative through adding the opposite; it does not mean every sentence containing two negative words becomes positive.
- **Confusing absolute value with sign:** distance is non-negative; the original number keeps its direction.

## Assessment

Given a temperature timeline and a bank-balance scenario, represent each event with signed numbers, calculate the final value and explain the reference point. **Independent standard:** signs match the direction of change, ordering is correct, and the learner distinguishes absolute value from signed value.

## Transfer task

Interpret a game score or inventory ledger that can move above and below a reference value. Define what zero means, encode gains and losses, calculate a sequence of changes and explain why the same signed arithmetic can describe different real-world meanings.

## Entry assumptions and next step

Entry: whole-number arithmetic and number-line familiarity. Next: algebraic expressions and equations. Complex numbers and formal number-system construction are outside scope.

# BASE-MATH-007 — Variables, expressions and algebraic structure

**Suggested workload:** 3–4 focused sessions of 35–50 minutes  
**Entry knowledge:** Arithmetic fluency, multiplication, signed numbers and basic operation order.

**Learning outcomes**
- Distinguish variables, expressions, terms, coefficients and equations.
- Translate verbal relationships into algebraic expressions.
- Combine like terms and apply the distributive property.
- Evaluate and verify equivalent expressions for selected inputs.


## What this module teaches

Algebra uses symbols to express quantities and relationships. A variable can represent an unknown value, a changing quantity, or a general number in a rule. It is not always one hidden number waiting to be discovered. For example, in d = vt, distance d changes with speed v and time t; the symbols represent related quantities.

An expression combines numbers, variables and operations but does not state that two quantities are equal. In 3x + 5, x is multiplied by 3 and then 5 is added. An equation states that two expressions have equal value, such as 3x + 5 = 20. A term is a part separated by addition or subtraction; the coefficient is the number multiplying a variable. Like terms have the same variable part with the same powers, so 3x and 5x can be combined, but 3x and 3x² cannot.

The distributive property a(b + c) = ab + ac explains how multiplication applies to every term inside parentheses. It also allows factoring in reverse. Algebraic simplification must preserve value for every allowed input; it is not merely making an expression shorter.

## Worked example 1 — Translate a relationship

A delivery costs ৳40 as a fixed charge plus ৳15 per kilometre. If k is the number of kilometres, total cost C is C = 40 + 15k. For k = 3, C = 40 + 45 = ৳85. The variable k can take different non-negative values; the expression describes the rule before a particular trip is chosen.

## Worked example 2 — Combine like terms

Simplify 4x + 3 + 2x − 7. Combine the x terms: 4x + 2x = 6x. Combine constants: 3 − 7 = −4. The result is 6x − 4. Check with x = 2: original expression = 8 + 3 + 4 − 7 = 8; simplified expression = 12 − 4 = 8. A numerical check supports the simplification, although the algebraic rule is the justification.

## Worked example 3 — Use the distributive property

Expand 3(2x − 5). Multiply 3 by both terms: 3 × 2x = 6x and 3 × (−5) = −15. The result is 6x − 15. A common error is to multiply only the first term; that would change the expression for almost every x.

## Guided practice

1. Write “a number n increased by 7, then doubled” as an expression. Be careful about the order.
2. Simplify 5a + 2a − 4 + 9.
3. Expand 4(y + 3).
4. Evaluate 2x² + 3 for x = −2, observing that the square is applied before multiplication.

**Guided answers:** (1) 2(n + 7). (2) 7a + 5. (3) 4y + 12. (4) 2 × 4 + 3 = 11.

## Independent exercise set

1. A phone plan costs ৳100 plus ৳8 per day. Write an expression for d days and calculate 12 days.
2. Simplify 7m − 3 + 2m + 8 − 4m.
3. Expand 5(2p − 3) and check it at p = 2.
4. Factor 6x + 12 by taking out the greatest common factor.
5. Explain why 2x + 3x² cannot be simplified to 5x³.

**Answer guidance:** (1) C = 100 + 8d; for 12 days, ৳196. (2) 5m + 5. (3) 10p − 15; at p=2 both original and expanded forms equal 5. (4) 6(x + 2). (5) They are unlike terms with different powers; addition does not combine exponents.

## Common errors and feedback

- **Treating every variable as a single unknown:** identify whether it is an unknown, changing quantity or general input.
- **Combining unlike terms:** combine only terms with identical variable parts and powers.
- **Applying distribution to only one term:** multiply every term inside the parentheses.
- **Confusing expression and equation:** an expression has a value; an equation asserts equality and can be solved or tested.

## Assessment

Translate two short real situations into expressions, simplify one expression, expand another and evaluate a formula for given inputs. **Independent standard:** symbols are defined, operation order matches the wording, transformations preserve value, and the learner can distinguish expression, equation and variable roles.

## Transfer task

Model the total cost of a small digital product order with a fixed platform fee and a per-unit cost. Define variables, express revenue and cost separately, and create an expression for gross difference. State which real-world costs are omitted so the model is not mistaken for net profit.

## Entry assumptions and next step

Entry: arithmetic, negative numbers, fractions and operation order. Next: equations, inequalities and linear models. Quadratics and functions are later extensions.

# BASE-MATH-008 — Equations, inequalities and preserving equality

**Suggested workload:** 3–4 focused sessions of 35–50 minutes  
**Entry knowledge:** BASE-MATH-006 and BASE-MATH-007, including signed arithmetic and algebraic expressions.

**Learning outcomes**
- Solve one-variable linear equations using equality-preserving operations.
- Solve linear inequalities and explain reversal when multiplying or dividing by a negative.
- Verify candidate solutions in the original statement.
- Interpret solution sets in context and distinguish equations with one, no or infinitely many solutions.


## What this module teaches

An equation states that two expressions have the same value. Solving an equation means finding all allowed values of the variable that make the statement true. The equals sign is a relationship, not an instruction to “write the answer next”.

The balance model helps explain why the same operation must be applied to both sides. If two sides have equal value, adding the same amount to each side preserves equality; so does subtracting the same amount, multiplying both sides by the same non-zero number or dividing both sides by the same non-zero number. Each step must be reversible or otherwise justified so solutions are not accidentally lost or added.

An inequality compares values using less than, greater than, less than or equal to, or greater than or equal to. Adding or subtracting the same value preserves its direction. Multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative reverses the direction because the order of negative numbers runs opposite to positive numbers. The solution may be a range rather than one number.

## Worked example 1 — Solve a two-step equation

Solve 3x + 5 = 20. Subtract 5 from both sides: 3x = 15. Divide both sides by 3: x = 5. Check in the original equation: 3(5) + 5 = 20, so the solution works.

## Worked example 2 — A contextual equation

A ride costs ৳50 to start plus ৳10 per kilometre. The total fare is ৳130. Let k be the distance. The equation is 50 + 10k = 130. Subtract 50: 10k = 80. Divide by 10: k = 8 km. The answer is meaningful because distance cannot be negative in this context. The model assumes a constant ৳10 per kilometre and no extra fees.

## Worked example 3 — Solve an inequality with a negative coefficient

Solve −2x > 8. Divide both sides by −2 and reverse the inequality sign: x < −4. Check with x = −5: −2(−5) = 10, and 10 > 8 is true. Check with x = 0: 0 > 8 is false. The number line solution contains all values less than −4, not only −5.

## Guided practice

1. Solve x + 9 = 14 and check by substitution.
2. Solve 4y = 28.
3. Solve 2a − 6 = 10.
4. Solve −3p ≤ 12 and explain why the sign changes when dividing.

**Guided answers:** (1) x = 5. (2) y = 7. (3) a = 8. (4) p ≥ −4; dividing by a negative reverses the inequality.

## Independent exercise set

1. Solve 5x − 7 = 18 and verify.
2. Solve 3(n + 4) = 27.
3. A number decreased by 6 is 19. Form and solve an equation; explain why the answer is 25.
4. Solve 2x + 3 < 11 and represent the solution in words.
5. Solve −4t > 20. Test one value that satisfies the solution and one that does not.

**Answer guidance:** (1) 5x=25, x=5; substitution gives 18. (2) n+4=9, n=5. (3) n−6=19, n=25. (4) x<4. (5) t<−5; t=−6 gives 24>20, while t=0 gives 0>20 false.

## 4. Equations can have one, no or infinitely many solutions

Not every linear equation has exactly one solution. Consider `2x+3=11`: subtracting 3 and dividing by 2 gives the single solution `x=4`. In `2(x+1)=2x+2`, expanding gives `2x+2=2x+2`; both sides are identical, so every real x is a solution (subject to any contextual domain restriction). In `2x+3=2x+7`, subtracting `2x` gives `3=7`, which is impossible, so there is no solution. These outcomes are called an identity and a contradiction, respectively.

For inequalities, a solution is a set of values. For example, `2x+3<11` gives `x<4`; on a number line this is every real number to the left of 4, with an open endpoint at 4. In interval notation this is `(-∞,4)`. Use interval notation only when the learner has met it; words and number lines are valid alternatives.

**Additional independent practice:** Classify each equation as one solution, no solution or infinitely many solutions: (a) `3x−2=10`; (b) `4x+1=4x−5`; (c) `5(x−1)=5x−5`.

**Answer:** (a) one solution, x=4; (b) no solution because 1=−5 is impossible; (c) infinitely many solutions because both sides simplify to `5x−5`.

## Common errors and feedback

- **Changing sides as a magic sign rule:** write the same inverse operation on both sides and show each line.
- **Doing an operation to only one side:** use the balance model; equality is preserved only when the transformation is valid on both sides.
- **Forgetting to reverse an inequality after multiplying/dividing by a negative:** test a value on the number line or use the ordering of negative numbers.
- **Ignoring context restrictions:** a mathematical solution may be invalid if the variable represents a non-negative count, distance or time.

## Assessment

Solve one two-step equation, one equation with parentheses, one inequality requiring division by a negative, and one contextual modelling problem. Show transformations, verify equation solutions in the original statement, represent inequality solutions as ranges and state domain restrictions. **Independent standard:** each operation is justified, signs/direction are correct, and the solution is interpreted in context.

## Transfer task

A small business has a fixed setup cost and a known contribution per sale. Write an inequality for the minimum number of sales needed to cover the fixed cost. Solve for the smallest whole-number sales count and explain why rounding down would not satisfy the condition. Keep the model's assumptions visible; this is a simplified break-even model, not a complete accounting statement.

## Entry assumptions and next step

Entry: arithmetic with signed numbers and algebraic expressions. Next: linear equations in multiple contexts, coordinate graphs and functions. Simultaneous equations and nonlinear inequalities are later extensions.
