# Wave 2 — Six-Discipline Teaching Pack, Batch 02 (Production Message 11)

**Status:** Authored working draft; internally checked after creation. Not expert-reviewed, learner-piloted or source-verified.

## Purpose and progression
This batch deepens the six Wave 2 disciplines introduced in Batch 01. It deliberately does not repeat the introductory unit topics. Each discipline receives two developing-level units, yielding a 12-unit progression set. These units are designed for external study followed by learner-generated evidence and independent evaluation; they are not intended as passive AI textbook consumption.

## How to use a unit
1. ATHENA assigns a unit only after checking prerequisites and the learner's current evidence.
2. The learner chooses a suitable external source (textbook, course, official documentation, credible lecture or supervised practice).
3. The learner records source title, author/publisher, exact chapter/section or timestamp, access date and claims relied upon.
4. The learner studies independently, then explains the concept without copying source prose.
5. The learner completes the task using fictional/synthetic data unless a safe, consented real context is explicitly appropriate.
6. An evaluator scores the evidence, asks targeted follow-up questions and records uncertainty.
7. ATHENA proposes a structured update; the learner previews and approves any commit. No unit completion or mastery is awarded merely because a lesson was read.

## Common scoring protocol
Each unit has five rubric dimensions scored 0–3 (0 absent/unsafe, 1 emerging, 2 competent with minor gaps, 3 robust and transferable). A provisional pass is 11/15; FIN requires 12/15; CYBER requires 12/15 and zero scope violations. These thresholds are formative design choices, not validated psychometric cut scores. A pass indicates performance on this task only; it does not automatically establish durable mastery. Require delayed retrieval or a second, varied application before considering a stable competency transition.

## Evidence quality rules
- Distinguish source claims, learner inference and evaluator feedback.
- Do not treat polished writing, confidence or AI-generated explanations as proof of understanding.
- Prefer a worked artifact plus oral/written defence over recall alone.
- Record sample size, assumptions and missing data.
- Preserve unsuccessful attempts and corrections; they are useful learning evidence.
- Never upload secrets, sensitive personal data or identifiable records when synthetic data will suffice.

## BIZ-PROG-001 — Offer design: value proposition, scope and customer fit

**Discipline:** BIZ  
**Progression:** developing  
**Estimated study:** 3–5 focused hours  
**Prerequisites:** BIZ-FOUND-001, basic_arithmetic, clear_written_communication

### Learning outcomes
- Translate a validated customer problem into a specific offer
- State target user, job, promised outcome, delivery mechanism and exclusions
- Distinguish a feature from a customer-valued benefit
- Test offer clarity without pressuring respondents
- Revise an offer when evidence contradicts an assumption

### Core model
An offer is a bounded promise to a defined customer: for whom, which problem or job, what outcome, by what mechanism, at what price or exchange, with what limits. A value proposition is not a slogan. It is a testable explanation of why this option is more useful than the next-best alternative for a particular segment. Use the chain: segment → situation/job → friction → desired outcome → offer mechanism → proof → constraints. Features describe what exists; benefits describe what changes for the customer. A feature matters only when a customer values the consequence. Avoid claiming guaranteed outcomes you cannot control.

### Worked example
A student seller offers “premium digital setup help.” That is vague. A clearer offer is “For small community owners who struggle to configure role permissions, a 30-minute guided setup session maps roles and tests access using a checklist; it excludes account recovery and any request for passwords.” The second version identifies user, task, mechanism, time boundary and exclusions.

### Method
1. Re-state the customer and problem using evidence from discovery. 2. Write the customer job in a verb phrase. 3. Define the smallest useful outcome. 4. List delivery steps and required inputs. 5. State exclusions, risks and support boundary. 6. Compare with alternatives: do nothing, DIY, competitor, substitute. 7. Draft a one-sentence value proposition. 8. Test comprehension with neutral questions (“What do you think this includes?”), not “Would you buy this?” 9. Record confusion and revise.

### Guided practice
Given a fictional group of student club organisers who lose track of event registrations, draft two competing offers: a spreadsheet template and a lightweight hosted form. For each, specify user, job, outcome, delivery, price assumption, proof needed, exclusions and likely alternative. Identify which claims are untested.

### Independent evidence task
Create an offer brief for a real or fictional low-risk project. Conduct at least three consent-based comprehension checks or clearly label a desk-research-only simulation. Do not collect unnecessary personal data or pressure anyone to buy. Submit the first draft, raw anonymised observations, revised draft and a change log.

### Evaluation rubric
Score 0–3 each: customer/problem specificity; outcome and mechanism coherence; honest scope/claims; evidence quality; revision logic. Strong work names disconfirming evidence and explains what it cannot yet promise.

### Common misconceptions
A catchy slogan is a value proposition; more features always add value; stated interest equals purchase intent; a guarantee can be used to compensate for weak proof; all users in a segment behave alike.

### Transfer and retention
Use the same offer logic for a school project proposal, a portfolio service, a volunteer initiative or an internal process improvement. Revisit after real delivery and compare promised versus experienced outcome.

### Safety and ethics boundary
No deceptive scarcity, fabricated testimonials, dark patterns, pressure selling or collection of sensitive data. For minors, involve a trusted adult in real transactions and follow platform/payment rules.

### Evidence submission contract
Submit: (1) source/provenance note with exact material studied, (2) explanation from memory, (3) worked application or artifact, (4) limitations/uncertainties, (5) confidence estimate, and (6) reflection on what changed after feedback. A source summary alone is not evidence of independent understanding.

## BIZ-PROG-002 — Unit economics: costs, contribution margin and break-even reasoning

**Discipline:** BIZ  
**Progression:** developing  
**Estimated study:** 3–4 focused hours  
**Prerequisites:** BIZ-PROG-001, FIN-FOUND-001, percentages_and_arithmetic

### Learning outcomes
- Classify fixed, variable, direct and indirect costs
- Calculate contribution per unit and contribution margin ratio
- Calculate simple break-even volume with stated assumptions
- Distinguish revenue, gross profit, contribution and cash
- Run sensitivity checks and identify missing cost categories

### Core model
Unit economics asks whether one repeatable unit of a product or service creates enough contribution to help cover fixed costs and, eventually, profit. Revenue = price × units sold. Variable cost changes with units; fixed cost stays broadly constant within a relevant range and period. Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit. Contribution margin ratio = contribution per unit ÷ selling price. Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit, rounded up to a whole unit. This simplified model assumes constant price and variable cost, one product mix, and a defined period. It does not automatically equal cash break-even or accounting profit.

### Worked example
A fictional template sells for ৳100. Payment and delivery costs are ৳12 per sale; other variable support cost averages ৳8. Contribution is ৳80. Monthly fixed costs are ৳1,200. Break-even = 1,200 ÷ 80 = 15 sales. At 20 sales, contribution after fixed costs is 20×80−1,200 = ৳400 before tax, owner labour not included, and any omitted costs. If a discount drops price to ৳85 while variable cost remains ৳20, contribution becomes ৳65 and break-even rises to 19 units (1,200÷65=18.46, round up).

### Method
1. Define product, unit, currency and time period. 2. List price and every cost triggered by one more sale. 3. Separate fixed costs from variable costs; flag mixed costs. 4. Calculate contribution. 5. List fixed costs for the same period. 6. Calculate break-even and round up. 7. Add owner time, refunds, failed delivery, platform fees, taxes and replacement/support where relevant. 8. Run downside/base/upside scenarios. 9. State assumptions and what would invalidate the model.

### Guided practice
A fictional service sells for ৳250. Each delivery costs ৳30, payment fee ৳10 and average support time is valued at ৳40. Monthly fixed cost is ৳2,400. Calculate contribution, margin ratio and break-even units. Then test a 10% price reduction and a 20% increase in delivery cost.

### Independent evidence task
Build a one-page unit economics sheet for a fictional or guardian-approved low-risk offer. Include price, unit definition, fixed/variable cost ledger, contribution, break-even, three scenarios, omitted-cost register and a plain-language interpretation. Do not use private bank or wallet credentials.

### Evaluation rubric
Score 0–3: cost classification; arithmetic; period/unit consistency; sensitivity analysis; transparent assumptions and omissions. A numerically correct answer with inconsistent periods cannot score above 1 on consistency.

### Common misconceptions
Revenue is profit; all costs are variable; owner time is free; break-even is a forecast; average contribution guarantees cash availability; one product mix formula fits every business.

### Transfer and retention
Apply to event budgets, fundraising, game-server hosting, print runs or a school club sale. For multiple products, learn weighted-average contribution and sales mix before applying a single break-even figure.

### Safety and ethics boundary
Educational modelling only, not investment, tax or legal advice. Do not share account balances, transaction IDs, passwords or private customer records.

### Evidence submission contract
Submit: (1) source/provenance note with exact material studied, (2) explanation from memory, (3) worked application or artifact, (4) limitations/uncertainties, (5) confidence estimate, and (6) reflection on what changed after feedback. ## FIN-PROG-001 — Financial statements: income, position and cash-flow logic

**Discipline:** FIN  
**Progression:** developing  
**Estimated study:** 4–6 focused hours  
**Prerequisites:** FIN-FOUND-001, addition_subtraction_percentages, record_classification

### Learning outcomes
- Distinguish an income statement, balance sheet and cash-flow statement
- Classify common transactions using a simple accounting equation
- Explain why profit and cash differ
- Reconcile a small fictional transaction set
- Identify timing, depreciation and non-cash limitations

### Core model
Three statements answer different questions. An income statement summarises performance over a period: revenue minus expenses produces profit or loss. A statement of financial position (balance sheet) shows assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time; Assets = Liabilities + Equity. A cash-flow statement explains cash movement over a period, commonly grouped into operating, investing and financing activities. Profit is not the same as cash: a sale on credit may create revenue before cash arrives; buying equipment uses cash but is not always expensed all at once; depreciation allocates an asset cost over time without a current cash payment.

### Worked example
A fictional shop begins with owner cash of ৳10,000. It buys inventory for ৳3,000 cash, sells goods that cost ৳1,200 for ৳2,000 cash, and pays ৳300 rent. Simplified period profit = sales 2,000 − cost of goods sold 1,200 − rent 300 = ৳500. Cash ends at 10,000−3,000+2,000−300 = ৳8,700. Remaining inventory is ৳1,800. The simplified balance sheet has cash ৳8,700 + inventory ৳1,800 = assets ৳10,500; no liabilities; equity ৳10,500 (opening capital 10,000 + profit 500).

### Method
1. Set an entity and reporting period. 2. Record each transaction with date, amount and evidence. 3. Ask which accounts change. 4. Maintain the accounting equation. 5. Separate earned revenue from cash received and expense incurred from cash paid. 6. Prepare a simple income statement. 7. Prepare closing assets/liabilities/equity. 8. Reconcile cash opening + inflows − outflows = closing cash. 9. Explain estimates and missing information.

### Guided practice
Use a fictional ledger: owner contributes ৳5,000; buys supplies for ৳600 cash; performs ৳1,500 work, receiving ৳1,000 now and ৳500 later; pays ৳200 transport. Calculate profit, closing cash, receivable and equity, stating whether supplies are consumed or still on hand.

### Independent evidence task
Create a three-statement mini-pack for a fictional microbusiness across one month. Include at least eight transactions, a transaction-to-account map, statement totals, reconciliation checks and a note explaining two profit/cash differences.

### Evaluation rubric
Score 0–3: correct statement purpose; transaction classification; arithmetic/reconciliation; period consistency; assumptions and explanation. Arithmetic errors must be corrected before interpreting ratios.

### Common misconceptions
Cash received always equals revenue; buying an asset is always an immediate expense; profit means money in the wallet; the balance sheet is a period total; every small project needs complex formal accounting.

### Transfer and retention
Use the logic to understand club budgets, project grants, household budgets and published company statements. Real reporting standards and local tax rules require qualified sources.

### Safety and ethics boundary
Fictional data preferred. Never upload personal financial statements, passwords, full account numbers or another person’s records. Educational accounting only.

### Evidence submission contract
Submit: (1) source/provenance note with exact material studied, (2) explanation from memory, (3) worked application or artifact, (4) limitations/uncertainties, (5) confidence estimate, and (6) reflection on what changed after feedback. A source summary alone is not evidence of independent understanding.

## FIN-PROG-002 — Risk, diversification, liquidity and time horizon

**Discipline:** FIN  
**Progression:** developing  
**Estimated study:** 3–5 focused hours  
**Prerequisites:** FIN-FOUND-001, FIN-PROG-001, probability_and_percentages

### Learning outcomes
- Distinguish risk capacity, tolerance and need
- Explain diversification and concentration risk
- Compare liquidity and time horizon
- Interpret nominal return versus inflation-adjusted return
- Identify scams, uncertainty and unsuitable claims

### Core model
Financial risk is not just price movement. It includes loss, volatility, liquidity, credit, operational, fraud and inflation risk. Risk capacity is the ability to absorb loss without damaging essential needs; risk tolerance is emotional willingness to endure uncertainty; risk need is the return required to reach a goal. Diversification spreads exposure across assets or sources of risk; it reduces some concentration risk but does not eliminate market-wide loss. Liquidity is how readily an asset can be converted to spendable money without a large loss. Match the time horizon and purpose of money to its liquidity and risk. Real return is approximately nominal return minus inflation for small rates; exact real return = (1+nominal)/(1+inflation)−1.

### Worked example
A fictional savings account grows by 6% while prices rise 8%. Exact real change = 1.06/1.08−1 ≈ −1.85%. The account balance increased in taka, but purchasing power fell. A person who needs money next month has a different liquidity constraint from someone investing for decades. A single asset or single customer source can create concentration risk even if its recent return looks attractive.

### Method
1. Name the goal and when the money is needed. 2. Separate essential reserves from money that can be exposed to loss. 3. Identify risk types, not just “high/low”. 4. Check liquidity, fees, lockups and counterparty. 5. Ask what happens in a downside scenario. 6. Examine concentration by asset, issuer, sector, geography and income source. 7. Compare nominal and real outcomes. 8. Reject guaranteed high-return claims and urgency tactics. 9. Seek a qualified adult/professional for real decisions.

### Guided practice
Compare three fictional options: cash reserve accessible today; a 12-month fixed deposit with early withdrawal penalty; and a volatile asset with no guaranteed buyer. A school trip fee is due in six weeks. Explain which risks matter most and why a higher advertised return does not settle the choice.

### Independent evidence task
Write a risk map for a fictional goal with a stated amount and date. Include risk capacity/tolerance/need distinctions, liquidity requirements, concentration risks, inflation, fees, fraud red flags and questions that remain unanswered. Do not recommend a real product or invest money.

### Evaluation rubric
Score 0–3: goal/time alignment; risk taxonomy; diversification nuance; real-return reasoning; fraud/uncertainty awareness.

### Common misconceptions
Diversification prevents all losses; high return means good choice; price volatility is the only risk; a long horizon guarantees recovery; “guaranteed” returns are trustworthy; nominal balance growth equals purchasing-power growth.

### Transfer and retention
Apply to emergency planning, business cash reserves, project continuity and career income concentration. The same resilience logic applies beyond investments.

### Safety and ethics boundary
This is conceptual financial education, not personalised investment advice. At the learner’s age, real financial products or accounts require guardian involvement and local legal compliance.

### Evidence submission contract
Submit: (1) source/provenance note with exact material studied, (2) explanation from memory, (3) worked application or artifact, (4) limitations/uncertainties, (5) confidence estimate, and (6) reflection on what changed after feedback. A source summary alone is not evidence of independent understanding.

## WORK-PROG-001 — Project planning: scope, dependencies, critical path and risk buffers

**Discipline:** WORK  
**Progression:** developing  
**Estimated study:** 3–5 focused hours  
**Prerequisites:** WORK-FOUND-001, task_decomposition, basic_time_estimation

### Learning outcomes
- Write a bounded project charter and definition of done
- Decompose outcomes into verifiable work packages
- Map finish-to-start dependencies
- Identify a critical path in a small network
- Estimate with ranges and add risk-based buffers
- Re-plan transparently when assumptions change

### Core model
A project is a temporary effort that creates a defined outcome under constraints. Start with a charter: purpose, deliverable, in-scope, out-of-scope, owner, stakeholders, constraints and acceptance criteria. Decompose the deliverable into work packages small enough to estimate and verify. A dependency says what must happen before something else can start or finish. The critical path is the longest-duration path through a dependency network; delay on it delays the earliest finish unless work is changed. Estimates are uncertain, so use ranges and explicit assumptions. Buffers should reflect uncertainty, not hide poor planning.

### Worked example
A 5-day mini-site project has tasks: requirements (1 day), content draft (2 days after requirements), visual design (2 days after requirements), build (3 days after design and content), test (1 day after build). Content and design run in parallel. Path requirements→design→build→test is 7 days; requirements→content→build→test is also 7 days. The schedule is at least 7 working days before buffer, not the sum of every task (9 days). If design slips, build cannot start.

### Method
1. Define one deliverable and acceptance test. 2. Set exclusions. 3. Break into tasks with observable outputs. 4. Estimate optimistic/likely/pessimistic durations. 5. Draw dependencies. 6. Find the longest dependency path. 7. Mark external approvals and resource constraints. 8. Add a named contingency for identified risks. 9. Set review points. 10. Re-baseline only with a reason and record the change.

### Guided practice
Plan a school exhibition with venue approval, poster design, materials purchase, setup, safety check and event. Identify parallel work, external dependencies and the task whose delay most threatens opening time.

### Independent evidence task
Create a project charter, work breakdown, dependency diagram, estimate ranges, risk register and weekly update for a real low-risk personal project or fictional case. Include one change request and explain its schedule/cost/scope effect.

### Evaluation rubric
Score 0–3: scope/acceptance clarity; decomposition; dependency logic; uncertainty estimation; change control and communication.

### Common misconceptions
A task list is a schedule; every task can run in parallel; the sum of durations is always project duration; buffers are wasted time; changing scope has no effect; dates without owners create accountability.

### Transfer and retention
Apply to website builds, electronics prototypes, study plans, events, video production and team projects. For complex projects, later study resource levelling, probabilistic estimation and portfolio dependencies.

### Safety and ethics boundary
Do not use real high-risk or costly projects as first exercises. No destructive changes to shared systems without explicit authority and backup.

### Evidence submission contract
Submit: (1) source/provenance note with exact material studied, (2) explanation from memory, (3) worked application or artifact, (4) limitations/uncertainties, (5) confidence estimate, and (6) reflection on what changed after feedback. A source summary alone is not evidence of independent understanding.

## WORK-PROG-002 — Process measurement, documentation and safe automation

**Discipline:** WORK  
**Progression:** developing  
**Estimated study:** 3–5 focused hours  
**Prerequisites:** WORK-FOUND-001, WORK-PROG-001, file_literacy

### Learning outcomes
- Map a repeatable process and identify its failure points
- Choose a meaningful process metric and baseline
- Write an operational checklist with owner and exception path
- Distinguish deterministic automation from judgment
- Design dry-run, logging, rollback and permission boundaries

### Core model
A process is a repeatable sequence that transforms inputs into outputs. Map trigger, input, steps, decision points, output, owner and exception path. Measure the outcome the process is meant to improve, not activity merely because it is easy to count. Useful measures may include cycle time, error rate, rework, completion rate and service quality; define numerator, denominator, time window and exclusions. Automation should be introduced only after the process is understood. A safe automation has least privilege, preview/dry-run, idempotency where possible, logs, bounded scope, error handling, backup and rollback. Keep human approval for irreversible or consequential actions.

### Worked example
A club manually copies registration data into a spreadsheet. Before automating, document who submits, which fields are required, how duplicates are handled, who can view data, and what happens when a form is incomplete. Baseline: median processing time and number of correction requests across a defined sample. A script that silently overwrites rows is not an improvement. A safer first version validates input, reports proposed changes, and requires approval before writing.

### Method
1. Observe the process without assuming the cause. 2. Draw current-state steps and handoffs. 3. Identify rework, wait time and failure modes. 4. Choose one outcome metric plus a guardrail metric. 5. Record baseline and sample limitations. 6. Simplify the process manually first. 7. Automate a reversible low-risk step. 8. Test normal, missing, duplicate and malformed inputs. 9. Log what changed. 10. Provide rollback and a human escalation path.

### Guided practice
For a fictional weekly file backup process, map source folders, destination, schedule, verification, retention and restore test. Identify what a “successful copy” fails to prove. Design a checklist that includes a sample restore.

### Independent evidence task
Document and measure a repeatable process. Propose one low-risk automation with a dry-run, permissions, validation, logs, failure response, rollback and human approval boundary. If no real process can be measured, label all data simulated.

### Evaluation rubric
Score 0–3: process map; metric validity; documentation completeness; automation safety; test/rollback quality.

### Common misconceptions
Automating a bad process fixes it; more metrics are always better; a green success message proves correctness; logs should contain all personal data; automation should have broad permissions; backups are valid without restore tests.

### Transfer and retention
Use for file organisation, publishing workflows, inventory, study review and business operations. Connect later to software testing, reliability engineering and data governance.

### Safety and ethics boundary
No mass messaging, account scraping, payment actions, deletion or production writes during practice. Use synthetic data and least-privilege test environments.

### Evidence submission contract
Submit: (1) source/provenance note with exact material studied, (2) explanation from memory, (3) worked application or artifact, (4) limitations/uncertainties, (5) confidence estimate, and (6) reflection on what changed after feedback. A source summary alone is not evidence of independent understanding.

## CYBER-PROG-001 — Identity and access: authentication, authorization and session security

**Discipline:** CYBER  
**Progression:** developing  
**Estimated study:** 4–5 focused hours  
**Prerequisites:** CYBER-FOUND-001, account_device_file_network_basics

### Learning outcomes
- Distinguish identification, authentication and authorization
- Explain password hashing versus encryption at a conceptual level
- Evaluate multi-factor authentication and recovery paths
- Describe session cookies, expiry and logout risks
- Apply least privilege and account recovery hygiene

### Core model
Identification claims an identity; authentication verifies control of an authenticator; authorization decides what an authenticated identity may do. Authentication factors are commonly something you know, have or are. MFA combines independent factors, although phishing-resistant methods are stronger than codes that can be relayed. Passwords should be unique and stored by services using slow salted password hashing, not reversible plaintext storage. A session token represents an authenticated session; it must be protected, scoped, expired and invalidated appropriately. Recovery is part of security: weak recovery can bypass strong login. Least privilege gives each user/service only the access needed.

### Worked example
A school club site has members, moderators and an owner. Authentication proves who logged in; authorization maps roles to allowed actions. A moderator may remove spam but cannot export all member records or change ownership. If a shared admin password is used, actions cannot be attributed and revocation is difficult. Separate accounts and scoped roles improve accountability.

### Method
1. Inventory accounts and what each protects. 2. Use unique passwords and a reputable password manager where appropriate. 3. Enable MFA, preferring phishing-resistant options when supported. 4. Secure recovery email/phone and backup codes. 5. Review active sessions and revoke unknown ones. 6. Apply least privilege to people and integrations. 7. Avoid sharing credentials or OTPs. 8. Check device lock, updates and screen privacy. 9. Practice account recovery using official provider guidance.

### Guided practice
Given a fictional app with viewer, editor, moderator and owner roles, create an authorization matrix for read profile, edit own profile, publish post, remove post, export records, manage billing and transfer ownership. Explain why role names alone do not guarantee secure implementation.

### Independent evidence task
Create a personal-account security plan using a fictional inventory. For each account, record importance, unique credential status, MFA, recovery path, active-session review and minimum access. Do not submit actual usernames, passwords, recovery codes or screenshots exposing secrets.

### Evaluation rubric
Score 0–3: terminology; factor/recovery reasoning; authorization matrix; session awareness; privacy/least privilege. Any request to reveal secrets is an automatic task failure.

### Common misconceptions
Authentication equals authorization; MFA makes phishing impossible; a session ends when a tab closes; hashing is encryption; recovery is an afterthought; administrators should share one account.

### Transfer and retention
Apply to websites, cloud drives, code repositories, game accounts and home routers. Advanced study may cover OAuth/OIDC, passkeys, secure cookie attributes and access-control testing in a lab.

### Safety and ethics boundary
Defensive education only. Never test login bypasses, steal tokens, phish, enumerate accounts or access another person’s account. Use owned test accounts and official documentation.

### Evidence submission contract
Submit: (1) source/provenance note with exact material studied, (2) explanation from memory, (3) worked application or artifact, (4) limitations/uncertainties, (5) confidence estimate, and (6) reflection on what changed after feedback. A source summary alone is not evidence of independent understanding.

## CYBER-PROG-002 — Network and web security fundamentals: boundaries, validation and safe testing

**Discipline:** CYBER  
**Progression:** developing  
**Estimated study:** 4–6 focused hours  
**Prerequisites:** CYBER-FOUND-001, CYBER-PROG-001, basic_web_and_network_concepts

### Learning outcomes
- Describe client, server, DNS, IP, port and HTTPS roles
- Distinguish transport protection from application authorization
- Explain input validation, output encoding and parameterized queries
- Recognize common web risk categories conceptually
- Design a safe test plan for an owned local lab

### Core model
A networked application has trust boundaries. DNS maps names to records; IP addresses route packets; ports identify services; HTTP carries application requests; HTTPS protects transport with TLS but does not prove that the application authorizes every action correctly. Validate input against expected type, size and allowed values; encode output for its context; use parameterized database queries rather than concatenating untrusted input. Server-side authorization must be checked for every protected operation. Client-side hiding is not access control. Security testing requires explicit scope, safe data and permission.

### Worked example
A profile page hides the “admin” button from ordinary users. If the server accepts an admin API request from any logged-in user, the control is cosmetic. Correct design checks the user’s role on the server for each operation, records the decision and denies by default. HTTPS protects traffic in transit but does not repair the authorization flaw.

### Method
1. Draw browser/client, network, server, database and third-party boundaries. 2. Mark data crossing each boundary. 3. Define expected input schema and size limits. 4. Validate server-side. 5. Encode output based on HTML/URL/JavaScript context. 6. Use parameterized queries and safe framework defaults. 7. Authorize every sensitive server action. 8. Log security events without secrets. 9. Test only a local toy app or explicitly authorized lab. 10. Document scope, test cases, results and remediation.

### Guided practice
Review a fictional contact form and role-protected dashboard. Identify risks from unbounded input, reflected text, direct object references, missing server authorization and verbose error messages. Propose mitigations without attempting exploitation.

### Independent evidence task
Build or diagram a local toy app security checklist: assets, trust boundaries, input schema, output contexts, authorization checks, error handling, dependency updates and test cases. Submit a scope statement proving the system is owned or intentionally provided as a lab.

### Evaluation rubric
Score 0–3: network model; trust-boundary reasoning; secure design controls; server-side authorization; scope and test discipline. Any out-of-scope testing is a zero and stops the exercise.

### Common misconceptions
HTTPS means the site is safe; frontend validation is sufficient; hidden buttons protect routes; all input should be trusted after login; security testing means trying random payloads on public sites.

### Transfer and retention
Apply to forms, dashboards, APIs, bots and local services. Further study should use intentionally vulnerable training apps and their published rules, never public targets.

### Safety and ethics boundary
No scanning, fuzzing, exploitation or credential testing on public IPs, school networks, shared hosting or third-party sites. Only local owned labs or explicitly authorized environments.

Submit: (1) source/provenance note with exact material studied, (2) explanation from memory, (3) worked application or artifact, (4) limitations/uncertainties, (5) confidence estimate, and (6) reflection on what changed after feedback. ## PSY-PROG-001 — Judgment and decision-making: bias, calibration and evidence

**Discipline:** PSY  
**Progression:** developing  
**Estimated study:** 3–5 focused hours  
**Prerequisites:** PSY-FOUND-001, basic_probability, observation_vs_interpretation

### Learning outcomes
- Distinguish a cognitive bias from a demonstrated error in a specific case
- Identify base-rate neglect, confirmation bias and availability effects
- Make calibrated probabilistic forecasts
- Use pre-mortems and disconfirming evidence
- Update a belief when new evidence arrives

### Core model
Human judgment uses shortcuts that are often efficient but can mislead. Availability makes vivid or recent examples feel more common; confirmation bias favours evidence that supports an existing belief; base-rate neglect underweights background frequencies. A bias label is not proof that a person is wrong. Calibration compares confidence with outcomes over many cases: among claims assigned 70% confidence, roughly 70% should be correct over a sufficiently large, well-defined set. Forecasting requires a clear resolution condition, time horizon and evidence update rule.

### Worked example
A learner says there is a 90% chance a video will reach 10,000 views because one previous video did. One success is a small and selected sample. A better forecast defines “reach 10,000 views within 30 days,” examines the full set of comparable uploads, accounts for base rates and updates when early retention data arrives.

### Method
1. State the question so it can resolve. 2. Record an initial probability and rationale. 3. Write the base rate or comparison class. 4. List evidence for and against. 5. Ask what observation would change your mind. 6. Use a pre-mortem to identify failure paths. 7. Separate confidence from importance. 8. Resolve forecasts consistently. 9. Review calibration across many predictions, not one.

### Guided practice
For five fictional project outcomes, assign probabilities, define deadlines and resolution criteria, then identify base-rate information and one disconfirming signal for each. Explain why “I feel certain” is not a measurement.

### Independent evidence task
Maintain a small forecasting journal of 10–20 low-stakes, non-sensitive predictions over time. Record probability, resolution date, outcome and brief review. If there are too few outcomes, treat calibration conclusions as preliminary.

### Evaluation rubric
Score 0–3: question clarity; base-rate use; balanced evidence; probability calibration discipline; update quality.

### Common misconceptions
Naming a bias proves someone is biased; confidence equals accuracy; one outcome validates a method; probabilities are weakness; a pre-mortem predicts the future; all disagreement is irrational.

### Transfer and retention
Use in project estimates, business hypotheses, study planning, sports predictions and research interpretation. Advanced work can include Bayesian updating and decision theory.

### Safety and ethics boundary
Not psychological diagnosis or personality assessment. Do not infer mental disorders, manipulate people, or use the journal to evaluate others. Keep predictions low-stakes and non-sensitive.

## PSY-PROG-002 — Motivation, habits and environment design

**Discipline:** PSY  
**Progression:** developing  
**Estimated study:** 3–4 focused hours  
**Prerequisites:** PSY-FOUND-001, goal_setting, basic_observation

### Learning outcomes
- Distinguish motivation, ability, cue and reinforcement
- Design a small behaviour with a clear trigger and minimum version
- Reduce friction and add useful environmental cues
- Use implementation intentions and feedback loops
- Evaluate a habit experiment without overclaiming causality

### Core model
Repeated behaviour is influenced by cues, opportunity, ability, expected outcomes, social context and reinforcement. A habit is not simply willpower. Implementation intentions link a situation to an action (“When X occurs, I will do Y”). Reduce friction for the desired action and increase friction for unwanted distractions without relying on shame. Define a minimum viable action that can be completed on a difficult day. Track behaviour with a simple measure and review whether the environment helped. A small self-experiment can inform personal adjustment but rarely proves a general psychological law.

### Worked example
“Study more” is vague. “After lunch, I put the phone outside reach, open the prepared math page and solve one problem for at least five minutes” defines cue, action and minimum. A visible checklist and pre-opened book reduce startup friction. If the plan repeatedly fails because the room is noisy, redesign the environment rather than conclude the learner lacks character.

### Method
1. Choose one behaviour, not a whole identity. 2. Define when/where it starts. 3. Make the first action tiny and observable. 4. Prepare materials in advance. 5. Remove one friction point. 6. Add a cue or implementation intention. 7. Track completion and context, not moral worth. 8. Review after a fixed period. 9. Adjust one variable at a time when feasible. 10. Protect sleep, health and realistic workload.

### Guided practice
Redesign a fictional learner’s evening study routine where distractions, unclear tasks and missing materials cause delays. Produce a cue-action plan, minimum version, environment change and non-punitive review rule.

### Independent evidence task
Run a 7–14 day low-stakes behaviour experiment. Record the planned cue, action, completion, context and one barrier. Compare the pattern before and after one environmental change; describe alternative explanations and avoid claiming causation from a tiny sample.

### Evaluation rubric
Score 0–3: behaviour specificity; cue/action design; friction reduction; measurement quality; humane interpretation and limits.

### Common misconceptions
Motivation must come first; missing a day means failure; a streak is the goal; self-control can replace sleep; one experiment proves causation; shame improves long-term adherence.

### Transfer and retention
Apply to retrieval practice, exercise preparation, creative work and household routines. Use professional help for persistent distress or impairment rather than treating a habit plan as clinical care.

### Safety and ethics boundary
No extreme schedules, sleep restriction, punishment, food restriction or coercive tracking. Stop if the experiment increases distress; this unit is not therapy.

### Evidence submission contract
Submit: (1) source/provenance note with exact material studied, (2) explanation from memory, (3) worked application or artifact, (4) limitations/uncertainties, (5) confidence estimate, and (6) reflection on what changed after feedback. A source summary alone is not evidence of independent understanding.

## LEAD-PROG-001 — Delegation and decision rights: ownership without micromanagement

**Discipline:** LEAD  
**Progression:** developing  
**Estimated study:** 3–4 focused hours  
**Prerequisites:** LEAD-FOUND-001, task_decomposition, respectful_communication

### Learning outcomes
- Delegate outcomes with constraints and authority
- Distinguish responsibility, accountability and approval
- Define decision rights and escalation thresholds
- Match task risk to autonomy and review cadence
- Avoid dumping unclear work or retaining hidden vetoes

### Core model
Delegation transfers a defined piece of work with enough authority, context and resources to complete it. It is not merely assigning a task while keeping every decision. Specify outcome, owner, constraints, decision rights, resources, deadline, quality bar, check-in and escalation trigger. Responsibility is doing the work; accountability is answerability for the result; approval is a specific control point. Autonomy should match competence, risk and reversibility. High-risk irreversible decisions need tighter review than reversible low-risk work.

### Worked example
A project lead asks a teammate to “handle the website.” That hides scope and authority. Better: “Own the event information page by Friday. You may edit copy and layout in the draft branch. Do not change payments, domain settings or publish without review. Ask for help if required content is missing by Wednesday.” The task has outcome, boundary, permission and escalation.

### Method
1. Define the outcome and acceptance criteria. 2. Select an owner and confirm capacity. 3. State decision rights explicitly. 4. Provide context, resources and constraints. 5. Agree on check-in cadence and escalation triggers. 6. Ask the owner to restate the plan. 7. Review evidence, not personal style. 8. Remove blockers. 9. Record decisions and hand back learning.

### Guided practice
Create a RACI-like responsibility map for a school event with venue, safety, publicity, budget and setup. Ensure every decision has one accountable owner and that approval requirements are explicit.

### Independent evidence task
Delegate one low-risk task in a group or simulate it. Submit the brief, authority boundary, acceptance criteria, check-in notes, outcome and reflection from both delegator and delegate perspectives where consent allows.

### Evaluation rubric
Score 0–3: outcome clarity; authority alignment; risk/reversibility fit; communication/check-in; respect and learning.

### Common misconceptions
Delegation means giving away blame; the leader must approve every detail; autonomy means no support; one owner means no collaboration; unclear work can be fixed by more reminders.

### Transfer and retention
Apply to group projects, client work, volunteer teams and software contributions. Advanced study can add governance, RACI variants, DACI and incident command.

### Safety and ethics boundary
No coercion, unpaid exploitation, public shaming or assigning work beyond someone’s capacity/authority. Respect consent and school/family rules.

Submit: (1) source/provenance note with exact material studied, (2) explanation from memory, (3) worked application or artifact, (4) limitations/uncertainties, (5) confidence estimate, and (6) reflection on what changed after feedback. ## LEAD-PROG-002 — Feedback, coaching and conflict resolution

**Discipline:** LEAD  
**Progression:** developing  
**Estimated study:** 4–5 focused hours  
**Prerequisites:** LEAD-FOUND-001, LEAD-PROG-001, active_listening

### Learning outcomes
- Separate observation, impact and interpretation
- Give specific behaviour-focused feedback with a next step
- Ask coaching questions without pretending to diagnose
- Classify conflict by task, process or relationship source
- Use a fair resolution process and escalate safety concerns

### Core model
Useful feedback is timely, specific, behaviour-focused and connected to an agreed goal. Separate observable facts from interpretation: “The report arrived two days after the agreed date” is observable; “you are lazy” is a character judgment. Describe impact, invite the other perspective and agree on a next action. Coaching helps a person reason through options; it is not imposing the coach’s answer. Conflict may concern task substance, process/roles or relationship dynamics. Resolve low-stakes disagreements by clarifying shared goals, facts, interests and options. Power imbalance, harassment, threats or safety concerns require trusted adult/appropriate authority escalation, not private mediation.

### Worked example
Instead of “You never communicate,” say: “The supplier change was not posted in the group before the order, so two people used the old plan. What happened from your perspective? Next time, can we post a change notice before placing the order?” This identifies event, impact, listening and specific future behaviour.

### Method
1. Choose a private, appropriate time. 2. State the shared goal. 3. Describe observable behaviour and impact. 4. Ask for the other perspective. 5. Check facts and assumptions. 6. Identify whether the issue is task, process or relationship. 7. Generate options and criteria. 8. Agree owner, action and review date. 9. Document only what is necessary. 10. Escalate safety or serious misconduct.

### Guided practice
Role-play a disagreement where two teammates disagree about design quality and deadline. One values consistency; the other values speed. Produce a neutral issue statement, interests of both sides, three options, decision criteria and a review point.

### Independent evidence task
Write a feedback-and-resolution record for a fictional or consented low-stakes disagreement. Include facts, impact, perspectives, issue type, agreed next step, unresolved points and whether escalation is required. Do not publish private details.

### Evaluation rubric
Score 0–3: factual specificity; listening/fairness; conflict diagnosis; actionable agreement; boundary and escalation judgment.

### Common misconceptions
Feedback is a personality verdict; conflict is always bad; neutrality means ignoring harm; coaching means telling someone what to do; all disputes should be mediated; apologies alone resolve repeated process failures.

### Transfer and retention
Apply to peer projects, client revisions, team retrospectives and classroom collaboration. Advanced study can include negotiation, restorative practices and organisational conflict systems.

### Safety and ethics boundary
Do not mediate abuse, threats, harassment or serious power-imbalance cases alone. Involve a trusted adult or appropriate authority. No retaliation or public humiliation.

### Evidence submission contract
Submit: (1) source/provenance note with exact material studied, (2) explanation from memory, (3) worked application or artifact, (4) limitations/uncertainties, (5) confidence estimate, and (6) reflection on what changed after feedback. A source summary alone is not evidence of independent understanding.
