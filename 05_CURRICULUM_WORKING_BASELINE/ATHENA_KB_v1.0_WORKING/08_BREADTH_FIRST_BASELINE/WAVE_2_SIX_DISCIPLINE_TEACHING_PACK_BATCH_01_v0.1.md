## BIZ-FOUND-001 — Business discovery: customer, problem and evidence

**Purpose.** Learn to investigate whether a real, specific group has a problem worth solving before spending time building or promoting a solution. This is discovery, not a sales pitch.

**Prerequisites.** Basic arithmetic; ability to distinguish an observation from an interpretation; respectful interviewing and consent.

### Learning outcomes
By the end, the learner can: (1) define a customer segment without making it impossibly broad; (2) describe a problem in the customer's context; (3) distinguish observed behaviour, recalled experience, stated preference and actual commitment; (4) write neutral interview questions; (5) summarise evidence including contradictions and uncertainty; and (6) decide what to test next without claiming proof from a tiny sample.

### Core model
A useful discovery record separates **who**, **situation**, **job or goal**, **friction**, **current workaround**, **frequency**, **consequence**, and **evidence**. A person saying “that's a great idea” is a compliment, not evidence of demand. A person describing a recent incident, showing a workaround, or voluntarily taking a meaningful next step is stronger evidence, but still does not establish a viable market by itself.

A segment is a group with a meaningful shared context, not merely “everyone who uses the internet.” Define it with observable characteristics relevant to the problem: task, setting, constraints, buying role, or frequency of need. Avoid sensitive personal data unless genuinely necessary and appropriately protected.

### Worked example
Weak hypothesis: “Students need an app.” Better hypothesis: “Learners preparing for a particular exam struggle to organise practice questions across subjects and currently switch between notebooks and messaging groups.” The second is still a hypothesis. Interview people about the last time this happened: “Walk me through how you prepared yesterday. Where did you lose time? What did you do next?” Avoid leading prompts such as “Wouldn't an app solve this?”

Suppose five interviews reveal: three describe the problem weekly, one rarely encounters it, and one says the main difficulty is not organisation but lack of reliable questions. Do not report “60% of the market wants our app.” Report the small sample, recruitment method, observed pattern, counterexample and next uncertainty.

### Method
1. Write a one-sentence problem hypothesis and list assumptions.
2. Select a narrow, reachable segment and explain why it is relevant.
3. Recruit ethically; explain purpose, voluntary participation and how notes will be used.
4. Ask about past behaviour before hypothetical futures. Ask one question at a time; do not defend the idea.
5. Record concise notes without unnecessary identifying details.
6. Code notes into repeated patterns, exceptions, workarounds and unanswered questions.
7. Choose a cheap next test that could disconfirm the hypothesis.

### Guided practice
Rewrite these leading questions neutrally: “Would you pay for my amazing study app?” and “Don't you hate how difficult budgeting is?” Example revisions: “How do you currently organise study materials?” and “Tell me about the last time you tried to track spending.”

### Independent task
Conduct or simulate three consent-based interviews using a fictional or low-stakes problem. Submit the segment definition, hypothesis, question guide, anonymised evidence table, two supporting observations, one disconfirming observation, limitations and next test. If real interviews are not appropriate, use clearly labelled fictional cases; never present invented interviews as real research.

### Assessment rubric (0–3 each)
- Segment specificity: broad/unsupported → relevant and operational.
- Question neutrality: leading → open, behaviour-focused and non-leading.
- Evidence handling: opinion treated as fact → observations and interpretations separated.
- Uncertainty: overclaims → limitations and alternatives stated.
- Next test: vague → inexpensive test with a result that could change the decision.

**Pass evidence:** at least 11/15, with no zero for ethics or evidence integrity. A pass means this task was demonstrated, not that the business idea is validated.

### Misconceptions
- “People like it” means they will buy: false.
- Three interviews represent a market: false.
- A negative response is useless: false; it may reveal a segment mismatch or a better problem definition.
- Discovery is permission to collect any personal data: false.

### Transfer and retention
Apply the method to a school workflow, a small digital product or a community service. After 2 days, reconstruct the distinction between stated preference and observed behaviour; after 1 week, critique a new interview guide; after 3 weeks, run a second small discovery cycle and compare what changed.

## FIN-FOUND-001 — Personal cash flow, budgeting and financial records

**Purpose.** Build financial literacy by distinguishing money received, money spent, money committed and money actually available. This unit teaches record-keeping and planning, not investment recommendations.

**Prerequisites.** Addition, subtraction, percentages and dates; basic spreadsheet or table literacy.

### Learning outcomes
The learner can: (1) maintain a dated cash ledger; (2) reconcile opening and closing balances; (3) distinguish income, expense, asset, liability and commitment; (4) build a realistic budget with irregular costs; (5) identify cash-flow timing risk; and (6) explain why nominal balances do not fully describe purchasing power.

### Core model
A cash ledger records events that actually happened. A budget is a forward-looking plan. They are related but not interchangeable. For a period:

**Closing cash = opening cash + cash inflows − cash outflows.**

A commitment is money expected to be paid later; it may not have left the account yet, but treating it as spendable can create a shortfall. A receivable is money someone owes you; it is not cash until collected. A liability is an obligation. An asset is a resource controlled that may provide future benefit. Avoid calling every item you own a liquid asset: resale value and access to cash differ.

### Worked example
Opening balance: ৳1,000. Received: ৳500. Paid transport: ৳120. Bought supplies: ৳180. Closing balance = 1,000 + 500 − 120 − 180 = ৳1,200. If ৳300 is already committed for an upcoming payment, uncommitted cash is ৳900. If a customer promises to pay ৳250 next week, that promise is not part of today's cash balance.

### Budget construction
1. Choose a period and opening balance.
2. List reliable inflows separately from uncertain inflows.
3. Record fixed, variable, irregular and one-off expenses.
4. Add commitments and due dates.
5. Compare planned totals with actual records.
6. Explain variances without hiding them; revise the plan where needed.
7. Keep a small contingency if possible, without assuming every learner has spare income.

For percentage change, use `(new − old) / old × 100%` when the old value is nonzero. Inflation means the general price level rises; a nominal balance can increase while purchasing power falls. A simplified real-growth approximation is nominal rate minus inflation rate for small rates; the more exact relationship is `(1 + nominal rate)/(1 + inflation rate) − 1`. State the period and assumptions.

### Guided practice
A learner starts with ৳750, receives ৳300, spends ৳90 and ৳160, and sets aside ৳200 for a known fee. Calculate closing cash and uncommitted cash. Answer: closing cash = ৳800; uncommitted cash = ৳600.

### Independent task
Create a fictional 30-day ledger with at least 12 dated transactions, one delayed payment, one commitment and one irregular expense. Reconcile it, create a budget, calculate variances and write a short explanation of the largest cash-flow risk. Use fictional data; do not upload private account credentials or sensitive financial records.

### Assessment rubric (0–3 each)
- Arithmetic and reconciliation.
- Classification of cash, commitment, receivable and expense.
- Budget realism and treatment of irregular costs.
- Interpretation of timing and purchasing power.
- Privacy and clear assumptions.

**Pass evidence:** at least 12/15 and exact reconciliation. This is a financial-literacy demonstration, not professional financial advice.

### Misconceptions
- Revenue equals profit or cash: no.
- A promised payment is available cash: no.
- A budget records what happened: no; the ledger does.
- Higher nominal balance guarantees greater purchasing power: no.

### Transfer and retention
Use the model for a fictional club budget or small project. Reconcile after 2 days from memory, review a new scenario after 1 week, and compare planned versus actual cash after 1 month.

## WORK-FOUND-001 — Workflow design, prioritisation and reliable execution

**Purpose.** Turn a desired outcome into a repeatable, observable workflow while avoiding performative busyness and unsafe automation.

**Prerequisites.** Basic file literacy; ability to describe a task and its intended result.

### Learning outcomes
The learner can: (1) define an outcome and its acceptance criteria; (2) decompose work into tasks and dependencies; (3) distinguish priority, urgency and effort; (4) identify bottlenecks and failure points; (5) design a checklist or standard operating procedure; and (6) review a workflow using evidence rather than impressions.

### Core model
A workflow is a sequence of states, actions, decisions, inputs, outputs and handoffs. A task is not complete because time was spent on it; completion requires an observable output meeting agreed criteria. A useful task record includes owner, action, expected output, due condition, dependency, risk and verification method.

Prioritisation should consider consequence, deadline, dependency, reversibility and effort. A simple priority score can help organise discussion but is not objective truth. Do not let a numeric score conceal safety, legal, ethical or wellbeing constraints. A bottleneck is the stage that limits throughput; making another stage faster may simply create a larger queue.

### Worked example
Goal: publish a product page. Tasks: confirm product facts → prepare images → draft copy → review claims → test mobile layout → verify contact path → publish → monitor. Publishing before checking price and contact details creates rework. The dependency graph matters more than a flat to-do list.

### Workflow design procedure
1. Define the user-visible outcome and acceptance criteria.
2. Observe the current process before redesigning it.
3. Map steps, handoffs, decisions, queues and rework loops.
4. Identify the highest-impact failure or bottleneck.
5. Change one controllable factor at a time where practical.
6. Add checks at consequential boundaries, not needless bureaucracy everywhere.
7. Pilot with a reversible, low-risk test.
8. Record results, exceptions and rollback steps.

A checklist should be short enough to use, specific enough to prevent omission, and designed around critical failure points. Automation should begin only after the manual process is understood. Before a bulk operation, preview its target set, preserve a backup or reversible state, test on sample data and verify the result.

### Guided practice
A team receives 20 requests but can process only five per day. Requests arrive without required details, causing repeated clarification. Identify the bottleneck and propose a change. Strong answer: define required intake fields and validate completeness before queue entry; monitor rejection/rework rate and processing time. Merely asking staff to “work faster” does not address the cause.

### Independent task
Choose a safe personal or school workflow. Produce a current-state map, desired outcome, acceptance criteria, bottleneck hypothesis, redesigned workflow, checklist, risk/rollback note and a before/after measure. Do not automate deletion, payments, account changes or other consequential actions for this exercise.

### Assessment rubric (0–3 each)
- Outcome and acceptance criteria.
- Dependency and handoff mapping.
- Bottleneck diagnosis supported by evidence.
- Reversible, proportionate intervention.
- Verification and learning loop.

**Pass evidence:** at least 11/15 and a testable outcome measure.

### Misconceptions
- Productivity means filling every minute: false.
- More steps always improve quality: false.
- Automation fixes a poorly understood process: often false.
- A priority matrix can decide ethical or safety questions: false.

### Transfer and retention
Apply to a small software release, study session or customer-support process. Recreate the workflow after 48 hours, inspect an exception after a week, and run a second improvement cycle after a month.

## CYBER-FOUND-001 — Threat modelling and personal security fundamentals

**Purpose.** Learn to reason about digital risk defensively: what needs protection, who or what could threaten it, how an attack might occur, and which safeguards reduce risk. Practice is restricted to accounts, devices and intentionally authorised labs.

**Prerequisites.** Basic device, account, file and network concepts; ability to follow a written procedure.

### Learning outcomes
The learner can: (1) identify assets and security goals; (2) distinguish threat, vulnerability, likelihood, impact and risk; (3) map trust boundaries and attack surfaces; (4) apply least privilege, MFA, updates and tested backups; (5) recognise common social-engineering patterns; and (6) write a prioritised, proportionate mitigation plan.

### Core model
An **asset** is something valuable (account, data, device, service). A **threat** is a potential cause of harm. A **vulnerability** is a weakness that could be exploited. **Risk** combines likelihood and impact in context; a simple qualitative matrix is a conversation aid, not a precise probability model. A control reduces likelihood, impact, exposure or recovery time.

Security goals often include confidentiality, integrity and availability. A recovery email, SIM, browser session or password manager may be part of the account's trust chain. A strong password on the primary account does not help if the recovery path is weak. Authentication asks who you are; authorisation determines what you may do. Least privilege grants only the permissions needed.

### Worked example
Asset: personal email account. Threat: attacker obtains access through a fake login page. Vulnerability: reused password and no second factor. Impact: password resets for other services. Controls: unique password stored in a reputable password manager, phishing-resistant MFA where available, secured recovery methods, session review and recovery-code storage offline. This reduces risk but does not make compromise impossible.

### Defensive threat-modelling procedure
1. Define scope and assets; exclude systems you do not own or have explicit permission to test.
2. Draw data flows and trust boundaries.
3. List plausible threat events and preconditions.
4. Estimate impact and likelihood qualitatively, explaining uncertainty.
5. Select controls by expected risk reduction, cost and usability.
6. Define detection and recovery steps.
7. Test backups and account recovery safely.
8. Review after a meaningful system or threat change.

### Guided practice
A fictional school club stores member contact details in a shared drive. Identify assets, who needs access, likely exposure paths and controls. A sound answer includes access limited by role, secure sharing, minimal data collection, removal of access when roles change, and a recovery plan. Public links are not a default convenience.

### Independent task
Threat-model a fictional personal website or test account. Submit a simple data-flow diagram, asset list, three threat scenarios, likelihood/impact rationale, controls, residual risks and recovery steps. If using a lab, confirm it is explicitly designed for training and stay within its scope. No scanning, credential testing, exploitation or persistence against third-party targets.

### Assessment rubric (0–3 each)
- Scope and authorisation.
- Asset/threat/vulnerability distinction.
- Trust-boundary analysis.
- Proportionate controls and least privilege.
- Recovery and residual-risk explanation.

**Pass evidence:** at least 12/15 and no scope/authorisation violation. Unsafe activity is an automatic stop, not a scoring opportunity.

### Misconceptions
- Antivirus alone makes a device secure: false.
- HTTPS proves a site is trustworthy: false.
- A successful login means every action should be permitted: false.
- A backup is useful without restore testing: unverified.

### Transfer and retention
Apply to a fictional small business or school portal. Reconstruct the threat model after 2 days, review a new phishing scenario after a week, and perform a safe backup-restore tabletop exercise after a month.

## PSY-FOUND-001 — Learning science: attention, memory and retrieval

**Purpose.** Understand selected cognitive processes relevant to learning and use evidence-informed study methods without turning psychology into diagnosis or simplistic productivity rules.

**Prerequisites.** Ability to describe a learning task and record observations; basic understanding of variables and fair comparisons.

### Learning outcomes
The learner can: (1) distinguish attention, working memory and long-term memory; (2) explain encoding, retrieval, forgetting and interference at an introductory level; (3) compare retrieval practice with passive rereading; (4) design spaced review; (5) interpret a small learning experiment cautiously; and (6) identify limits of self-experimentation and individual variation.

### Core model
Attention selects information for processing; it is limited and influenced by goals, novelty, fatigue and environment. Working memory supports currently active information but is constrained. Long-term memory stores knowledge that can be retrieved and used; expertise can make complex patterns easier to process because related elements are organised into meaningful chunks.

Learning is not identical to feeling familiar. Rereading can increase fluency without ensuring unaided recall. **Retrieval practice** means trying to recall or use information before checking the answer. **Spacing** distributes practice over time. Feedback helps correct errors. Interleaving can help learners discriminate between problem types, although it may feel harder than blocked practice. No single technique works identically for every topic or learner.

### Worked example
A learner rereads ten vocabulary words five times and feels confident. Another closes the notes, attempts to recall the meanings, checks errors, and repeats the attempt on later days. The second learner may experience more effort but gains evidence about what can actually be retrieved. The correct comparison requires similar material, time and testing conditions; one anecdote is not a universal proof.

### Practical study cycle
1. Set a specific objective: explain, solve, compare or perform.
2. Study a small coherent section with attention to meaning.
3. Close the source and retrieve key ideas or solve a representative problem.
4. Check against a reliable source and correct errors.
5. Schedule another retrieval attempt after a delay.
6. Mix in previously learned material when useful.
7. Apply knowledge to a new example.
8. Track performance and confidence separately.

A simple schedule might revisit material later the same day, after 1 day, 3 days, 1 week and 2–3 weeks. Adjust based on difficulty and observed recall; this is a starting heuristic, not a biological law. Sleep, health, stress, accessibility and available time matter. Avoid shame-based streaks or treating missed sessions as character failure.

### Guided practice
After studying a short explanation of a concept, write a closed-book summary, list uncertainties, then compare with the source. Mark each statement as correct, incomplete or incorrect and revise it. The goal is accurate retrieval, not a polished first attempt.

### Independent task
Design a one-week comparison using two small, similar sets of low-stakes facts or concepts. Use retrieval on one set and rereading plus normal review on the other; keep study time similar, alternate order if possible, and test immediately and after a delay. Record the method, scores, confidence, limitations and what you would change. Do not make claims about intelligence or diagnose attention disorders from this exercise.

### Assessment rubric (0–3 each)
- Accurate distinction among memory processes.
- Correct description of retrieval and spacing.
- Fairness and transparency of comparison.
- Interpretation with limitations.
- Practical plan that is sustainable and non-punitive.

**Pass evidence:** at least 11/15 and no unsupported clinical claim.

### Misconceptions
- Learning should always feel easy: false.
- A high confidence rating guarantees recall: false.
- One study trick is best for everyone and every task: false.
- A short self-experiment diagnoses a psychological condition: false.

### Transfer and retention
Use the cycle for mathematics, language or technical vocabulary. Reconstruct it after 2 days, analyse a delayed recall result after a week, and refine the plan after a month.

## LEAD-FOUND-001 — Goal setting, execution and accountable teamwork

**Purpose.** Learn to coordinate a small project through clear outcomes, roles, decision rights, checkpoints and respectful review. Leadership is a set of behaviours and responsibilities, not a title or permission to control others.

**Prerequisites.** Ability to define a task and communicate respectfully; basic planning and record-keeping.

### Learning outcomes
The learner can: (1) translate a broad aim into a verifiable outcome; (2) decompose it into milestones and dependencies; (3) assign roles with authority and resources; (4) create a decision and communication cadence; (5) give behaviour-specific feedback; and (6) review outcomes and adapt without scapegoating.

### Core model
A useful goal specifies the outcome, beneficiary, evidence of completion, constraints and review date. A milestone is a verifiable intermediate result, not an activity such as “work on design.” Each work item should have an owner, deliverable, acceptance criteria, dependencies and escalation route.

Responsibility without authority or resources is a setup for failure. Delegation clarifies what result is expected, which decisions the person can make, what boundaries apply, what support exists and when to check in. Accountability means making commitments and outcomes visible, including leadership's own decisions; it does not mean humiliation or surveillance.

### Worked example
Weak goal: “Make the event successful.” Stronger goal: “By Friday, publish an accessible event information page with confirmed date, location, schedule, contact route and approval from the event owner.” Milestones: confirm facts → draft page → accessibility and mobile check → owner review → publish → verify. If location confirmation is a dependency, the team should surface it early rather than blame the page designer for waiting.

### Execution cycle
1. Agree on the outcome and why it matters.
2. Define acceptance criteria and non-negotiable constraints.
3. Break work into deliverables and dependencies.
4. Assign owners, decision rights, resources and support.
5. Choose a proportionate check-in cadence.
6. Keep a decision log for consequential choices.
7. Raise blockers early and distinguish facts from assumptions.
8. Review results, process and lessons; update the plan.

Feedback should describe an observable behaviour, its effect, a question or invitation to respond, and a specific next step. Example: “The revised file arrived after the review window, so the team could not test it before release. What blocked the handoff, and what checkpoint would help next time?” This is more actionable than “You are unreliable.”

### Guided practice
A three-person team must produce a two-page guide in five days. One member controls source facts, another drafts, another reviews. Create milestones and identify dependencies. A strong plan gives the source owner a deadline, reviewer criteria, a fallback if facts are late, and a final approval owner.

### Independent task
Plan a small, safe project (for example, a study resource or club information page). Submit a one-page charter, milestone board, role/decision-rights table, risk list, communication plan, decision log template and retrospective questions. Do not assign tasks to people who have not agreed to participate.

### Assessment rubric (0–3 each)
- Outcome and acceptance criteria.
- Dependencies and realistic milestones.
- Roles, authority and support.
- Feedback and conflict handling.
- Review and adaptation.

**Pass evidence:** at least 11/15, with clear authority and consent boundaries.

### Misconceptions
- Leadership means always deciding: false.
- Delegation means transferring blame: false.
- More meetings guarantee alignment: false.
- A missed milestone proves laziness: unsupported; investigate dependencies and constraints.

### Transfer and retention
Apply to a small software feature, group assignment or community activity. Rebuild the milestone plan after 48 hours, practise a feedback conversation after a week, and complete a retrospective after the project.
