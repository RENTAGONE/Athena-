## DEEP-MATH-001 — Number systems, magnitude and estimation

**Discipline:** Mathematics & Scientific Reasoning  
**Objective:** Represent integers, fractions, decimals, percentages and rational values; compare magnitude and estimate before calculating.

### Conceptual model
A number is a value, while its written form is a representation. Integers, fractions, terminating/repeating decimals and percentages can represent the same or comparable magnitudes. Estimation is an independent reasoning operation: establish a plausible range before exact calculation, then compare the answer with that range. A number line gives a common spatial model; place value explains decimal magnitude; equivalent fractions explain why different forms can denote the same value.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** Compare 0.7 and 0.65 without treating the digit strings as whole numbers.
**Reasoning:** Write 0.7 as 0.70. Both now have hundredths: 70 hundredths is greater than 65 hundredths. On a number line, 0.70 lies to the right of 0.65.
**Result:** 0.7 > 0.65.

**Problem:** Estimate 19% of 248, then calculate exactly.
**Reasoning:** 10% is about 25 and 20% is about 50, so the answer should be near 50. Exact calculation: 0.19 × 248 = 47.12. This fits the estimate.
**Result:** Approximately 47; exact value 47.12.

### Guided practice
- Place −1.5, −0.25, 0, 0.4 and 1.25 on one number line; explain why left/right represents order.
- Convert 3/8 to a decimal and percentage. Show the division and explain why the forms are equivalent.
- Estimate 15% of 390 using 10% + 5%, then calculate and compare.

### Independent exercise bank
- Order 0.8, 3/4, 0.72 and 78% from least to greatest.
- A price of ৳640 is discounted by 12.5%. Estimate the discount, calculate it, and state the sale price.
- A measurement is reported as 2.04 m. Explain why 2.4 m is not a harmless rearrangement.
- Construct two different fractions equal to 0.6 and justify them.
- Create a real-life estimate where rounding is useful and state the acceptable error range.

### Common failure modes
- Comparing decimal strings as whole numbers (e.g. 0.35 > 0.4 because 35 > 4).
- Adding denominators when adding fractions without finding equivalent units.
- Treating a percentage as a fixed quantity rather than “per hundred”.
- Giving an exact-looking answer without checking whether its scale is plausible.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Basic counting and place value
- Four operations with whole numbers
- Meaning of equal groups and division

### Follow-on candidates
- Ratio, rate and proportional reasoning
- Signed operations and rational arithmetic
- Measurement, unit conversion and uncertainty

### Diagnostic placement item
Which is larger, 0.4 or 0.35? Explain using place value or a number line. Then estimate 19% of 200. This distinguishes magnitude understanding from memorised procedures.

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Can perform a familiar conversion with prompts but cannot explain equivalence or magnitude reliably.
- **Developing:** Compares common forms and estimates simple percentages; occasional place-value or unit errors remain.
- **Independent:** Converts and compares mixed forms, estimates before calculating, explains method and checks plausibility in unfamiliar routine contexts.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Chooses an efficient representation, handles multi-step contextual quantities, quantifies reasonable error and explains limitations.

### Retention and transfer
Conceptual-mathematical: spaced retrieval should alternate representation, comparison, estimation and contextual application. Increase interval only after accurate explanation and transfer; shorten after magnitude errors.

**Transfer task:** Estimate the total cost of a multi-item purchase with a percentage discount and tax/fee if specified. Separate known values from assumptions, calculate, and explain why the estimate is a useful error check.

## DEEP-MATH-002 — Algebraic structure and equations

**Discipline:** Mathematics & Scientific Reasoning  
**Objective:** Translate verbal relationships into expressions, simplify using equivalence and solve linear equations while preserving equality.

### Conceptual model
Algebra represents relationships with symbols. A variable is a quantity whose value may vary or be unknown; an expression describes a quantity; an equation asserts that two expressions have equal value. Solving an equation means finding values that make the equality true. Equality is preserved by applying the same valid operation to both sides. This balance model is safer than “moving terms across the equals sign”, which hides why sign changes occur.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** Solve 3x + 5 = 20.
**Reasoning:** Subtract 5 from both sides: 3x = 15. Divide both sides by 3: x = 5. Check in the original: 3(5)+5 = 20.
**Result:** x = 5.

**Problem:** A notebook costs x taka. Three notebooks and a ৳20 delivery fee cost ৳170. Model and solve.
**Reasoning:** The total is 3x + 20 = 170. Subtract 20: 3x = 150. Divide by 3: x = 50. Substitute: 150 + 20 = 170.
**Result:** Each notebook costs ৳50.

### Guided practice
- Evaluate 2a + 3 when a = 4; distinguish substitution from solving.
- Solve x − 7 = 12 using inverse operations and check.
- Solve 4x = 36, then explain why division by 4 is valid on both sides.

### Independent exercise bank
- Simplify 3x + 2x − 4 + 7. State why only like terms combine.
- Solve 5x − 8 = 27 and verify by substitution.
- Translate “twice a number decreased by nine is 17” into an equation and solve.
- A phone plan costs ৳100 base plus ৳4 per GB. Write a cost expression for g GB and evaluate at 8 GB.
- Invent an equation with solution x = 6 and explain how you know.

### Common failure modes
- Treating the equals sign as “the answer comes next” rather than equality.
- Combining unlike terms (x + x²).
- Changing one side only when transforming an equation.
- Failing to check a solution in the original equation.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Four operations and order of operations
- Equality and inverse operations
- Basic use of symbols

### Follow-on candidates
- Ratios and proportional equations
- Linear inequalities
- Simultaneous equations and formula rearrangement

### Diagnostic placement item
Explain the difference between 4x + 2 and 4x + 2 = 18. Can either be “solved” without additional information?

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Can substitute into simple expressions but confuses expressions and equations or loses equality during solving.
- **Developing:** Solves one- or two-step linear equations with a known procedure; checks inconsistently.
- **Independent:** Builds equations from short contexts, solves using valid equivalence transformations and verifies solutions.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Chooses variables and models multi-step constraints, interprets solution meaning/units, and identifies impossible or extraneous results.

### Retention and transfer
Procedural-conceptual: mix solving, error diagnosis, equation construction and substitution checks. Retrieval should require explaining why each transformation preserves equality.

**Transfer task:** Model a small business break-even situation with fixed cost and per-item cost. Define variables, write an equation or inequality, solve, and interpret whether the result is realistic.

## DEEP-MATH-003 — Functions, graphs and mathematical modelling

**Discipline:** Mathematics & Scientific Reasoning  
**Objective:** Interpret a function as a mapping; connect tables, equations, graphs and contextual rates.

### Conceptual model
A function maps each allowed input to exactly one output. A table, rule, graph and verbal description are different representations of the same relationship. The independent variable is chosen or controlled; the dependent variable responds. A graph shows pattern and rate of change, but its scale, domain and context determine what can legitimately be inferred. A model is an approximation with assumptions, not reality itself.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** A ride costs ৳30 fixed fee plus ৳12 per kilometre. Express cost C for distance d and find C(5).
**Reasoning:** The fixed fee remains when d = 0; each kilometre adds 12. Thus C(d)=30+12d. At 5 km: 30+60=90.
**Result:** C(d)=30+12d; C(5)=৳90.

**Problem:** For y = 2x + 1, find outputs for x = 0, 1, 2 and describe the graph.
**Reasoning:** Substitution gives y = 1, 3, 5. Each increase of 1 in x increases y by 2; the graph is a straight line with vertical intercept 1.
**Result:** (0,1), (1,3), (2,5); constant rate of change 2.

### Guided practice
- Complete a table for y = 3x − 2 using x = −1, 0, 1, 2.
- Plot points with labelled axes and an even scale; explain what one grid step means.
- Compare two phone data plans: fixed fee + per-GB charge. Identify where one becomes cheaper.

### Independent exercise bank
- Describe a function in words, table and equation for a real repeated cost.
- Given a graph, identify intercepts, increasing/decreasing intervals and what the axes measure.
- Explain why a straight-line model may fail for battery percentage over time.
- Create a two-plan comparison and solve for the break-even input.
- Find a misleading graph scale and explain how it distorts visual interpretation.

### Common failure modes
- Confusing x and y roles or swapping axes.
- Assuming every relationship is linear.
- Extending a model outside its supported domain.
- Reading slope as a y-value rather than a rate of change.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Coordinate plane and ordered pairs
- Substitution into expressions
- Ratio/rate as “per unit”

### Follow-on candidates
- Linear and nonlinear models
- Statistics and data fitting
- Systems of equations and optimisation

### Diagnostic placement item
If y = 2x + 1, what is y when x = 3, and what does the 2 mean? A correct number without interpretation is partial evidence.

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Can substitute values but struggles to connect rule, table and graph.
- **Developing:** Builds and reads simple linear representations; interpretation of rate/domain is incomplete.
- **Independent:** Moves between representations, interprets slope/intercept with units and checks model fit.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Compares competing models, identifies assumptions and domain limits, and communicates uncertainty in predictions.

### Retention and transfer
Representation switching: retrieve a relationship from a graph, then generate graph/table/equation from context. Include non-examples to test function and linearity boundaries.

**Transfer task:** Collect a small real dataset (e.g. cost by quantity), graph it, propose a model, test a prediction against an observed value and state why the model may not generalise.

## DEEP-ENG-001 — Programming language fundamentals and control flow

**Discipline:** Engineering & Computer Science  
**Objective:** Write small programs using values, types, input/output, selection, iteration and functions; trace execution precisely.

### Conceptual model
A program is an explicit procedure executed by a machine according to language rules. Values have types; expressions compute values; variables bind names to values; control flow determines which statements execute and how often. A reliable beginner workflow is: state the intended behaviour, identify inputs/outputs, write the smallest procedure, run it on normal and boundary cases, inspect errors, and revise. Syntax correctness is necessary but not sufficient: a program can run and still implement the wrong logic.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** Read a user age and classify it as under 13, 13–17 or 18+.
**Reasoning:** Parse input as an integer before numeric comparison. Test boundaries 12, 13, 17 and 18. Use mutually exclusive if/elif/else branches so exactly one category is selected.
**Result:** Python: age=int(input("Age: ")); if age < 13: ... elif age < 18: ... else: ...

**Problem:** Calculate the total of three entered prices.
**Reasoning:** Input arrives as text, so convert each value to a numeric type. Sum values only after conversion. Decide whether decimal currency needs decimal-safe representation for a real financial application.
**Result:** For learning: sum(float(input(...)) for _ in range(3)); for real money use integer minor units or Decimal.

### Guided practice
- Trace a short sequence of assignments by hand and record each variable after every line.
- Write a branch that classifies a number as negative, zero or positive; test all three cases.
- Use a loop to sum 1 through n; trace n=1 and n=4 before running.

### Independent exercise bank
- Write a unit converter with input validation and clear units.
- Build a number guessing loop with a finite attempt limit and feedback.
- Write a function that accepts a list and returns its minimum; define behaviour for an empty list.
- Find and fix a type error, a boundary-condition bug and an infinite-loop bug in supplied snippets.
- Explain the difference between syntax, runtime and logic errors with one example each.

### Common failure modes
- Assuming input() returns a number.
- Off-by-one loop boundaries.
- Using == where assignment is required or vice versa.
- Testing only one happy-path input.
- Treating a program that runs as proof that it is correct.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Basic computational problem decomposition
- Arithmetic and comparison operators
- Text input/output

### Follow-on candidates
- Collections and data structures
- Functions/modules and testing
- Files, APIs, databases and event-driven programs

### Diagnostic placement item
Predict the output of a loop with a changing counter and explain when it stops. Then identify the type returned by input() in Python.

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Can write straight-line statements with guidance; struggles with types and control flow.
- **Developing:** Writes basic branches and loops but misses boundary cases or input validation.
- **Independent:** Decomposes a small problem, writes readable functions, tests normal/boundary/invalid inputs and explains control flow.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Chooses suitable data representations, handles errors, separates concerns and independently debugs an unfamiliar small task.

### Retention and transfer
Coding is procedural and generative: alternate tracing, code-from-scratch, debugging and modifying unfamiliar examples. A copied solution is not independent evidence.

**Transfer task:** Build a small personal expense or inventory tracker using only local input/output. Specify behaviour first, test edge cases, and document known limitations.

## DEEP-ENG-002 — Data structures, algorithms and complexity

**Discipline:** Engineering & Computer Science  
**Objective:** Choose arrays/lists, maps, sets, stacks and queues based on operations; reason about correctness and growth.

### Conceptual model
A data structure organises information so operations have defined costs and semantics. An algorithm is a finite, precise procedure that transforms input into output. Complexity describes how resource use grows with input size; Big-O is an asymptotic upper-growth description, not a stopwatch prediction. Choose structures by required operations and constraints, not fashion. Correctness comes before optimisation; measure real bottlenecks before making code more complex.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** Find whether a name occurs in an unsorted list of n names.
**Reasoning:** A linear scan checks items until a match or end. In the worst case it inspects n items, so time grows O(n). If the list is sorted, binary search can halve the remaining interval each step, giving O(log n), but sorting has a cost and ordering must be maintained.
**Result:** Linear search O(n); binary search O(log n) on sorted data.

**Problem:** Count frequency of each word in a sentence.
**Reasoning:** A map/dictionary associates each word with a count. For each token, increment its key. Average lookup/update is typically near O(1) in common hash-map implementations, though worst-case guarantees depend on implementation.
**Result:** Use a frequency map; time is expected O(n) over n tokens.

### Guided practice
- Trace linear search and count comparisons for a match at the beginning, middle and end.
- Use a dictionary to count repeated items and explain why a list of pairs would be less convenient.
- Compare nested loops over n items and a single loop; derive growth in simple terms.

### Independent exercise bank
- Choose a structure for a queue of support requests and justify required operations.
- Implement duplicate detection; state time and space costs.
- Compare list membership with set membership conceptually and experimentally on increasing input sizes.
- Explain why O(n log n) eventually grows faster than O(n), but small inputs can behave differently in practice.
- Design tests that would reveal an off-by-one error in binary search.

### Common failure modes
- Calling O(1) “one second”.
- Applying binary search to unsorted data.
- Ignoring memory/space complexity.
- Optimising before correctness or measurement.
- Treating average-case hash behaviour as an unconditional worst-case guarantee.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Loops and conditional logic
- Lists/arrays and key-value mappings
- Basic logarithm intuition or repeated halving

### Follow-on candidates
- Sorting/searching families
- Trees, heaps, graphs and hash tables
- Database indexing and system performance

### Diagnostic placement item
For a list of 100 unsorted values, can binary search be used directly? Explain the precondition, not just the algorithm name.

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Recognises common structures but cannot justify preconditions or estimate growth.
- **Developing:** Selects basic structures and describes linear/logarithmic growth with prompting.
- **Independent:** Chooses structures based on operations, traces algorithms, reasons about time/space and tests correctness.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Compares trade-offs under realistic constraints, identifies bottlenecks and supports optimisation with measurement.

### Retention and transfer
Requires repeated implementation and reasoning. Retrieve algorithm invariants and complexity explanations, then re-implement after delay without copying.

**Transfer task:** Design a searchable catalogue for products or learning resources. Define expected queries, choose structures, test correctness and explain the trade-off between lookup speed and update cost.

## DEEP-ENG-003 — Software design, testing and version control

**Discipline:** Engineering & Computer Science  
**Objective:** Separate responsibilities, define interfaces, test behavior and use version history to make changes reversible.

### Conceptual model
Software design controls complexity by separating responsibilities, defining interfaces and making behaviour testable. A test is an executable check of an expected property; it is not proof of all correctness. Version control records changes as a recoverable history, enabling comparison and collaboration. A useful cycle is small change → focused test → inspect diff → commit with meaningful message → integrate. Design should make likely changes local rather than scattering duplicated logic.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** Three buttons use the same contact number. A client changes the number. How should the code be structured?
**Reasoning:** Store the contact number in one configuration source and have each button use it. Add a test or search-based check that no stale hard-coded number remains. This reduces inconsistent updates.
**Result:** Single source of truth + regression check.

**Problem:** A function should return the larger of two numbers.
**Reasoning:** Specify behaviour for equal inputs as well as greater/less cases. Write tests for (2,5), (5,2), (4,4), and possibly negative values. Then implement and run tests.
**Result:** A complete test set checks normal and boundary cases, not only one example.

### Guided practice
- Refactor duplicated constants into one configuration object and inspect the diff.
- Write tests for a function before or immediately after implementation, including boundary cases.
- Create a branch, make one small change, commit it and inspect the history.

### Independent exercise bank
- Identify a module with too many responsibilities and propose a separation by interface.
- Write a test table for a discount calculator including zero, boundary, invalid and maximum values.
- Resolve a deliberate merge conflict in a practice repository.
- Explain the difference between a commit, branch, merge and remote repository.
- Review a diff and identify unrelated changes that should be separated.

### Common failure modes
- Large commits that mix unrelated changes.
- Testing only the visible happy path.
- Duplicating configuration across components.
- Assuming version control is a backup or that a pushed commit is automatically correct.
- Changing architecture without a concrete requirement.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Programming fundamentals and functions
- Basic file/folder and command-line navigation
- Understanding of expected vs actual behaviour

### Follow-on candidates
- Automated testing and CI
- API and database boundaries
- Architecture, deployment and operational reliability

### Diagnostic placement item
A feature works on your device but fails after a later change. What evidence would help isolate the regression? Name tests, diffs and environment details.

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Can make edits but lacks repeatable tests or recoverable change history.
- **Developing:** Uses commits and basic tests; tests miss boundaries or changes are poorly isolated.
- **Independent:** Defines behaviour, tests relevant cases, keeps changes focused and uses version control to inspect/revert work.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Designs clear interfaces, diagnoses regressions from evidence and manages a small feature through reviewable commits.

### Retention and transfer
Workflow skill: rehearse complete small change cycles periodically. Review quality is judged by reproducibility and diagnosis, not by commit count.

**Transfer task:** Take a small existing project, document one bug, reproduce it, add a regression test, fix it in a focused commit and write a concise change note.

## DEEP-ELEC-001 — Electrical safety, measurement and circuit models

**Discipline:** Electronics & Hardware  
**Objective:** Distinguish voltage, current, resistance and power; select measurement ranges and safe circuit procedures.

### Conceptual model
Electrical work begins with hazard control, not component experimentation. Voltage is potential difference, current is charge flow, resistance opposes current, and a circuit requires a complete path. A schematic is a symbolic model; a physical circuit may differ because of polarity, wiring errors, component tolerances and measurement loading. A multimeter reading is meaningful only when the meter mode, range, lead placement and circuit state are correct. This unit is restricted to low-voltage, current-limited educational circuits; mains electricity and high-energy sources are excluded.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** A battery-powered LED circuit does not light. What should be checked first?
**Reasoning:** Disconnect power before changing wiring. Verify battery polarity, LED polarity, series resistor presence/value, breadboard row continuity, and whether the supply is within component ratings. Do not bridge the supply with an ammeter or short circuit.
**Result:** Inspect unpowered wiring and ratings first; then measure safely with correct meter setup.

**Problem:** What does a 3.0 V reading across a component mean?
**Reasoning:** It is the potential difference between the two probe points at that moment and under that circuit condition. It does not by itself tell current unless resistance or another circuit relation is known.
**Result:** A voltage measurement is a difference between two nodes, not a direct current reading.

### Guided practice
- Identify voltage, current and resistance symbols on a simple schematic.
- With a disconnected, low-voltage practice circuit, plan where voltage would be measured in parallel.
- Create a pre-power checklist: source rating, polarity, exposed conductors, current limit, and no short circuit.

### Independent exercise bank
- Draw a battery-resistor-LED schematic and label current direction conventionally.
- Explain why an ammeter is placed in series and a voltmeter in parallel.
- Given a meter dial scenario, identify why measuring resistance on an energised circuit is unsafe/invalid.
- Diagnose an open circuit vs short circuit from symptoms without energising a questionable setup.
- Record a measurement with units, instrument, range, circuit condition and uncertainty.

### Common failure modes
- Measuring resistance on a powered circuit.
- Connecting an ammeter directly across a source.
- Ignoring polarity and component ratings.
- Treating a schematic as a photograph of breadboard rows.
- Assuming a zero reading always means “no voltage” rather than checking meter setup.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Basic arithmetic and units
- Understanding of complete paths and polarity
- Read and follow equipment instructions

### Follow-on candidates
- Ohm’s law and network analysis
- Component datasheets and tolerances
- Diodes, transistors and digital logic

### Diagnostic placement item
Why must a current meter not simply be placed across a battery? Explain the circuit path and likely consequence.

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Recognises basic symbols but cannot plan safe measurements.
- **Developing:** Explains basic quantities and meter placement but needs supervision for setup checks.
- **Independent:** Plans and records low-voltage measurements safely, checks polarity/ratings and interprets readings in context.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Diagnoses simple circuit faults systematically, accounts for measurement limits and refuses unsafe/unknown energy sources.

### Retention and transfer
Safety-critical procedural learning: no unsupervised progression to higher-energy systems. Refresh safety checklist before each practical session; require observed correct setup, not only written recall.

**Transfer task:** Create a low-voltage circuit inspection sheet for a supervised project. Include source limits, polarity, measurement plan, likely faults and a stop-work rule for unknown or damaged equipment.

## DEEP-ELEC-002 — Series/parallel networks and Ohm’s law

**Discipline:** Electronics & Hardware  
**Objective:** Apply V=IR and power relations to simple DC networks; distinguish branch and total quantities.

### Conceptual model
Ohm’s law relates voltage, current and resistance for an ohmic element under stated conditions: V = IR. In a series path, the same current flows through each element and voltage drops add. In parallel branches, each branch has the same voltage and branch currents add at a node. Equivalent resistance follows these constraints. These models assume ideal connections and components sufficiently close to the stated model; real components heat, vary and may be nonlinear.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** A 220 Ω resistor is connected to a 5 V ideal supply. Find current.
**Reasoning:** I=V/R=5/220 A≈0.0227 A. Convert to milliamps: about 22.7 mA. Check resistor power P=VI≈0.114 W; a 1/8 W resistor would be close to its rating, so choose a suitable margin rather than assuming any resistor is safe.
**Result:** Approximately 22.7 mA; power about 0.114 W.

**Problem:** Two 100 Ω resistors in series are connected to 6 V.
**Reasoning:** Series resistance is 200 Ω. Current is 6/200=0.03 A. Equal resistors split voltage equally, so each drops 3 V.
**Result:** 30 mA total; 3 V across each resistor.

### Guided practice
- Use V=IR to solve for each missing variable, keeping units consistent.
- Calculate total resistance for two series resistors and predict current.
- For two parallel branches, explain why voltage is shared but current divides.

### Independent exercise bank
- A 9 V source feeds 1 kΩ: calculate current and resistor power.
- Two 220 Ω resistors in parallel: calculate equivalent resistance.
- A 12 V source feeds 1 kΩ and 2 kΩ in series: calculate current and each voltage drop.
- Explain why adding a parallel branch can increase total source current.
- Compare ideal calculation with a measured value and list at least three plausible sources of difference.

### Common failure modes
- Using series rules on parallel branches.
- Mixing mA and A or kΩ and Ω.
- Applying V=IR to a nonlinear component without checking its operating region.
- Ignoring power dissipation and resistor rating.
- Assuming the supply maintains ideal voltage under any load.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Electrical safety and measurement
- Fractions, ratios and unit conversion
- Basic circuit diagrams

### Follow-on candidates
- Voltage dividers and Kirchhoff laws
- LED current limiting and power budgets
- Transistor biasing and sensor circuits

### Diagnostic placement item
A 10 Ω resistor and a 20 Ω resistor are in series. Which has the larger current? Which has the larger voltage drop? Explain.

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Can recall V=IR but confuses series/parallel constraints.
- **Developing:** Solves single-loop and simple parallel calculations with occasional unit or topology errors.
- **Independent:** Analyses basic mixed networks, checks current/voltage consistency and calculates power.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Explains model assumptions, compares measurements with predictions and identifies rating/tolerance constraints before construction.

### Retention and transfer
Quantitative-procedural: alternate diagram interpretation, equation selection, unit conversion and physical plausibility. Use fresh circuit topologies rather than repeated identical sums.

**Transfer task:** Design a low-voltage LED indicator circuit from supplied component ratings. Calculate current and power, choose safe margins, and verify the expected measurements under supervision.

## DEEP-ELEC-003 — Semiconductors: diodes and transistors

**Discipline:** Electronics & Hardware  
**Objective:** Explain junction behavior, polarity, switching and basic transistor control within datasheet limits.

### Conceptual model
Semiconductors are materials whose electrical behaviour can be controlled by doping, junctions and applied fields. A diode is not simply a one-way ideal switch: its current depends strongly on voltage and temperature, and its forward drop is not a universal fixed value. A transistor uses a control input to regulate a larger current or voltage behaviour; BJT and MOSFET devices differ in control mechanism and operating limits. Datasheets define pinout, maximum ratings and operating conditions. Never infer pin order from package appearance alone.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** Why does an LED need a current-limiting resistor in a simple voltage-source circuit?
**Reasoning:** An LED’s current rises steeply once forward biased. A resistor limits current approximately by R=(Vs−Vf)/I. For Vs=5 V, Vf≈2 V and target I=10 mA, R≈300 Ω; choose a standard value at or above this and verify power/current against the LED and supply ratings.
**Result:** About 300 Ω calculated; choose a suitable standard value such as 330 Ω after checking ratings.

**Problem:** What must be known before wiring an unknown transistor?
**Reasoning:** Identify exact part number, device type, pinout, maximum voltage/current/power, gate/base drive requirements, thermal limits and intended operating region from its datasheet. Package shape alone is insufficient.
**Result:** Exact part and datasheet, not visual guesswork.

### Guided practice
- Sketch a diode symbol and identify anode/cathode; distinguish conventional current direction from electron flow.
- Read a real LED datasheet and locate forward-current and forward-voltage specifications.
- Compare BJT base-current control with MOSFET gate-voltage control conceptually; do not assume interchangeable pinouts.

### Independent exercise bank
- Calculate a current-limiting resistor for a specified low-voltage LED using provided Vf and target current.
- Explain reverse bias and why a diode’s reverse-voltage rating matters.
- Use a datasheet to identify a transistor pinout and absolute maximum ratings.
- Explain why a transistor used as a switch must be driven into an appropriate on-state and checked for dissipation.
- Diagnose why a circuit may fail if the LED or transistor is reversed.

### Common failure modes
- Treating forward voltage as exactly constant in all conditions.
- Assuming all TO-92 or SOT packages share pinouts.
- Confusing absolute maximum ratings with recommended operating conditions.
- Ignoring thermal dissipation and base/gate drive.
- Using a transistor as a magic current amplifier without a defined circuit model.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Low-voltage safety and measurement
- Ohm’s law and resistor power
- Basic polarity and circuit diagrams

### Follow-on candidates
- BJT/MOSFET switching circuits
- Logic-level interfacing and microcontrollers
- Sensors, drivers and power electronics (with supervision)

### Diagnostic placement item
If a datasheet gives a maximum collector current, does that mean the transistor can safely carry that current in every circuit? Explain at least two other limits.

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Recognises diode/transistor symbols but relies on memorised pin guesses.
- **Developing:** Explains basic junction and switching behaviour; uses datasheet with prompts.
- **Independent:** Selects and checks a device from its datasheet, calculates LED limiting resistance and explains operating constraints.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Evaluates a simple switching design across electrical and thermal limits, identifies uncertainty and validates assumptions before construction.

### Retention and transfer
Conceptual plus safety: use datasheet interpretation as retrieval, and revisit ratings/pinout verification before each new component family. No live mains or high-current experimentation.

**Transfer task:** Select a transistor for a low-voltage indicator or small signal task using a provided datasheet. Produce a rating checklist and explain why the selected device is suitable; practical build only with supervision.

## DEEP-RES-001 — Question formation and operational definitions

**Discipline:** Research & Information Intelligence  
**Objective:** Turn broad curiosity into answerable questions with defined terms, population, context and scope.

### Conceptual model
A research question defines what is being investigated and what evidence could answer it. A broad topic becomes researchable when scope, population/context, variables or concepts, time frame and outcome are specified. Operational definitions turn abstract ideas into observable measures. A good question is neither so broad that evidence cannot resolve it nor so narrow that it has no meaningful answer. The question should determine method; do not choose a convenient method first and force the question to fit.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** Turn “Does studying help?” into a researchable question.
**Reasoning:** Specify learner group, study method, comparison, outcome and period. Example: among class-eight learners studying a defined topic, does spaced retrieval practice compared with rereading produce higher delayed quiz scores after seven days? Define “spaced retrieval”, quiz format and scoring before collecting data.
**Result:** A bounded comparative question with operational definitions.

**Problem:** Operationalise “phone distraction”.
**Reasoning:** A vague label needs observable indicators. Possible measure: number of self-initiated non-task app switches during a 30-minute study session, recorded with a stated counting rule. This captures one behaviour, not every meaning of distraction.
**Result:** Count of non-task app switches per 30-minute session, with limitations stated.

### Guided practice
- Take a broad claim and specify who, where, what, when and outcome.
- Write two different operational definitions for “physical activity” and compare what each misses.
- Identify whether a question is descriptive, comparative, causal or interpretive.

### Independent exercise bank
- Rewrite “Are online classes good?” into one descriptive and one comparative question.
- Define a measurable indicator for “website usability” and explain its limits.
- Choose a method that can answer a question and identify one method that cannot.
- Separate a research question from a prediction/hypothesis.
- Write inclusion/exclusion criteria for a small, ethical, non-sensitive observation task.

### Common failure modes
- Questions too broad to answer with available evidence.
- Operational definitions that silently change the meaning of the concept.
- Confusing correlation/comparison with causation.
- Collecting data before deciding what counts as a valid observation.
- Using sensitive personal data without necessity or safeguards.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Distinguish claim, evidence and opinion
- Basic variable and measurement concepts
- Clear sentence formulation

### Follow-on candidates
- Research design and sampling
- Measurement reliability/validity
- Evidence synthesis and causal inference

### Diagnostic placement item
What evidence could answer “Is Python easy?” Why is “easy” not yet measurable or clearly bounded?

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** States a topic or opinion but not a bounded, answerable question.
- **Developing:** Adds scope and a measurable outcome; method alignment or construct validity remains weak.
- **Independent:** Writes answerable questions, operationalises key concepts and matches methods to claims.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Compares alternative operational definitions, anticipates bias/ethics and states what the design cannot establish.

### Retention and transfer
Methodological: revisit by redesigning questions from new domains. Assess whether the learner can state what would count as evidence and what conclusions remain unavailable.

**Transfer task:** Design a small study about a learning technique using only voluntary, non-sensitive data. State question, definitions, sample limits, procedure, outcome and ethical safeguards before collecting anything.

## DEEP-RES-002 — Source evaluation, provenance and claim tracing

**Discipline:** Research & Information Intelligence  
**Objective:** Evaluate authorship, evidence, methods, incentives, recency and corroboration; trace claims to primary evidence.

### Conceptual model
A source is not automatically reliable because it looks professional, ranks highly or agrees with prior belief. Evaluation is claim-specific: inspect author expertise, publisher/editorial process, evidence, methods, date, conflicts, corrections and whether the source actually supports the claim. Provenance records where information originated and how it changed. Claim tracing separates a primary finding from later summaries and commentary. Triangulation means comparing independent evidence, not counting repeated copies of one source.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** A blog says “a study proves X” and links to a news article. How should the claim be traced?
**Reasoning:** Open the news article, identify the named study, locate the original paper or authoritative report, inspect what was measured and whether the result is causal. Check whether the blog exaggerates the population, effect or certainty. Record the exact claim and source chain.
**Result:** Trace back to primary evidence and compare the wording with the actual method/results.

**Problem:** Three websites repeat the same statistic. Is that three independent confirmations?
**Reasoning:** Check whether all cite one report or press release. If they share a single upstream source, they are repetitions, not independent evidence.
**Result:** No; shared provenance means one evidence origin.

### Guided practice
- Annotate a claim with author, date, publisher, evidence and limitation.
- Follow citations from a summary to its primary source and record each step.
- Compare a primary study abstract with a media headline; mark claims strengthened or omitted.

### Independent exercise bank
- Evaluate two sources making the same health/technology claim using a claim-specific checklist.
- Build a provenance chain for a statistic and identify the earliest accessible source.
- Identify a conflict of interest and explain why it matters without assuming it automatically invalidates results.
- Distinguish primary, secondary and tertiary sources in a given research trail.
- Write a short source note with supported claim, evidence type, date and limitation.

### Common failure modes
- Using domain prestige as a substitute for inspecting evidence.
- Treating citations as proof that the cited source supports the statement.
- Counting copied claims as independent corroboration.
- Ignoring publication date in rapidly changing fields.
- Calling a source “biased” without identifying a concrete mechanism or conflict.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Research question and claim distinction
- Basic web/search literacy
- Understanding of evidence vs assertion

### Follow-on candidates
- Evidence synthesis and uncertainty
- Systematic searching and reproducibility
- Scientific paper and data appraisal

### Diagnostic placement item
If five articles cite one press release, how many independent sources do you have? What would you inspect next?

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Checks surface cues but cannot trace the claim or assess evidence relevance.
- **Developing:** Finds original sources and notes date/author; independence and method appraisal are inconsistent.
- **Independent:** Traces claims, records provenance and evaluates methods, relevance, conflicts and limitations.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Maps multi-source evidence lineage, detects citation laundering and explains how provenance affects confidence.

### Retention and transfer
Epistemic habit: practice on claims from different domains. Require source trails and independent-source checks; refresh rapidly for time-sensitive subjects.

**Transfer task:** Choose a current non-sensitive claim in technology or science. Trace it to the best available original evidence, map repeated citations and produce a source note that clearly separates what is supported from what is not.

## DEEP-RES-003 — Evidence synthesis and uncertainty

**Discipline:** Research & Information Intelligence  
**Objective:** Synthesize multiple sources while preserving disagreement, limitations and uncertainty.

### Conceptual model
Evidence synthesis combines findings while preserving differences in quality, method, population and context. It is not a vote or a pile of quotations. First extract each source’s question, method, sample, result and limitations; then compare convergence, disagreement and gaps. Uncertainty can arise from measurement error, sampling, confounding, model assumptions, publication bias or limited generalisability. Conclusions should be calibrated to the evidence and updated when better evidence appears.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** Two small studies report different effects. What can be concluded?
**Reasoning:** Compare populations, measures, interventions, timing and uncertainty before deciding they conflict. One may measure short-term recall while another measures long-term transfer. If methods differ, the results may answer different questions.
**Result:** Do not average blindly; explain comparability and unresolved uncertainty.

**Problem:** A survey of 30 volunteers finds 70% prefer an app. Can this represent all learners?
**Reasoning:** The sample is small and self-selected; the result describes respondents, not necessarily the target population. Selection bias and uncertainty limit generalisation.
**Result:** It is a descriptive result for that sample, not a population estimate without stronger sampling.

### Guided practice
- Create an evidence table with question, method, sample, finding, limitation and relevance.
- Group sources into agreement, disagreement and not directly comparable.
- Rewrite an overconfident conclusion using calibrated language.

### Independent exercise bank
- Synthesize three sources on one bounded question without treating them as equal quality.
- Explain at least two reasons a result may not generalise beyond its sample.
- Distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of absence in a scenario.
- Identify a confounder in an observational comparison.
- Write a conclusion with confidence level, key limitations and what new evidence would change it.

### Common failure modes
- Counting studies instead of weighing quality and comparability.
- Treating non-significance as proof of no effect.
- Overgeneralising from convenience samples.
- Confusing correlation with causation.
- Hiding uncertainty in a confident summary.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Question formation and operational definitions
- Source evaluation and provenance
- Basic proportions/statistics vocabulary

### Follow-on candidates
- Systematic reviews and meta-analysis concepts
- Causal inference and experimental design
- Decision-making under uncertainty

### Diagnostic placement item
A study finds no statistically clear difference. Does that prove the two methods are identical? Explain what else you need to know.

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Summarises sources individually but does not compare their evidence.
- **Developing:** Identifies agreement and limitations but gives an uncalibrated overall conclusion.
- **Independent:** Synthesises comparable evidence, explains disagreement and states bounded conclusions.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Assesses evidence strength and uncertainty across methods, proposes discriminating future evidence and updates conclusions proportionately.

### Retention and transfer
Judgement skill: use fresh mixed-quality evidence sets. Score calibration, traceability and limitations, not whether the learner guesses the eventual consensus.

**Transfer task:** Prepare a one-page evidence brief comparing several sources on a practical question. Include a conclusion, evidence table, limitations, confidence statement and what would change the conclusion.

## DEEP-LANG-001 — Phonology, listening discrimination and pronunciation

**Discipline:** Language & Linguistic Intelligence  
**Objective:** Perceive and produce relevant sound contrasts using listening, articulatory awareness and feedback.

### Conceptual model
Speech is a coordinated stream of sounds shaped by articulators, timing, stress, rhythm and intonation. Phonemes distinguish words within a language; accents vary systematically and are not defects. Listening discrimination means noticing contrasts in real speech, including reduced forms and connected-speech changes. Pronunciation training should prioritise intelligibility, stable sound contrasts and listener understanding rather than imitation of one prestige accent. Recording and comparing one’s own speech can make progress observable.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** Distinguish /ɪ/ and /iː/ in “ship” and “sheep”.
**Reasoning:** The contrast includes vowel quality and duration, but duration alone is not a universal rule. Listen to multiple speakers and use the word context. Record both words in a short carrier phrase and compare whether a listener identifies the intended word.
**Result:** The target is reliable lexical contrast and intelligibility, not exaggerated length.

**Problem:** Why can “want to” sound different in fluent speech?
**Reasoning:** Connected speech includes reduction and linking; function words may be unstressed and sounds may assimilate or disappear in casual pronunciation. Recognition improves by hearing phrases, not only isolated dictionary forms.
**Result:** Train phrase-level listening alongside careful citation forms.

### Guided practice
- Listen to minimal-pair recordings from varied speakers and mark the word heard before seeing the transcript.
- Record a short sentence, identify one target contrast and repeat slowly then naturally.
- Mark stressed syllables and thought groups in a short spoken passage.

### Independent exercise bank
- Build a personal list of five sound contrasts that affect intelligibility and collect example words.
- Transcribe a short clip, compare with transcript and note reductions/linking.
- Record the same 30-second passage weekly and track one measurable feature.
- Explain the difference between phoneme, syllable, stress and intonation.
- Ask a listener to transcribe a short phrase and use misunderstandings as evidence.

### Common failure modes
- Treating accent difference as error.
- Practising isolated sounds without checking words and connected speech.
- Over-focusing on speed or loudness.
- Assuming spelling predicts pronunciation consistently.
- Using one recording as proof of stable improvement.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Ability to hear and repeat short phrases
- Basic syllable awareness
- Familiarity with target language sound inventory

### Follow-on candidates
- Connected speech and listening comprehension
- Prosody and public speaking
- Second-language phonological acquisition

### Diagnostic placement item
Can a listener reliably distinguish your pronunciation of two words that differ by one sound? Test with a minimal pair rather than self-rating alone.

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Can repeat isolated sounds but cannot reliably hear or produce target contrasts in words.
- **Developing:** Improves selected contrasts in rehearsed phrases; spontaneous speech remains inconsistent.
- **Independent:** Discriminates and produces target contrasts in new words and connected speech with intelligibility.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Self-diagnoses recurring pronunciation issues, adapts to listener feedback and maintains intelligible speech across topics and speaking rates.

### Retention and transfer
Perceptual-motor: short frequent practice with varied speakers, delayed re-recording and listener feedback. Avoid high-volume strain; stop if discomfort occurs.

**Transfer task:** Record a short explanation of a topic without reading. Have a listener note words they could not identify, then choose one sound/prosody target for the next practice cycle.

## DEEP-LANG-002 — Vocabulary depth, morphology and retrieval

**Discipline:** Language & Linguistic Intelligence  
**Objective:** Learn words with meaning, form, collocations, morphology, register and active retrieval.

### Conceptual model
Knowing a word deeply involves more than recognising a translation. Useful dimensions include form, pronunciation, core meaning, grammatical behaviour, collocations, register, connotation and ability to retrieve it in context. Morphology reveals how prefixes, roots and suffixes relate word families, but word formation is language-specific and not every apparent pattern is productive. Retrieval practice strengthens access; spaced encounters across varied contexts support flexible use.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** Distinguish “decide”, “decision” and “decisive”.
**Reasoning:** The root meaning is related, but grammatical roles differ: decide is a verb, decision a noun, decisive an adjective. Sentence frames expose the difference: “They decide”; “the decision”; “a decisive result”.
**Result:** Word-family knowledge includes form and syntax, not only shared translation.

**Problem:** Learn “reliable” beyond a translation.
**Reasoning:** Record pronunciation, adjective category, meaning (can be depended on), collocations (reliable source/person/method), opposite (unreliable), register and an original sentence. Retrieve it later without looking.
**Result:** A multidimensional entry supports actual use.

### Guided practice
- Build a word-family map from a known root and verify each form in a dictionary/corpus.
- Use a new word in two contexts and explain whether its meaning/register shifts.
- Retrieve yesterday’s words from prompts before reviewing the answers.

### Independent exercise bank
- Create a vocabulary entry for ten useful academic words with pronunciation, part of speech, collocation and original sentence.
- Identify a false friend or misleading cognate in a language you study.
- Choose the correct word form for a sentence and explain the grammatical clue.
- Write a short paragraph using five target words naturally; then revise awkward collocations.
- Design a spaced retrieval plan based on accuracy and difficulty, not a fixed streak.

### Common failure modes
- Memorising only one-word translations.
- Learning a word without part of speech or collocation.
- Assuming suffix patterns work identically across languages.
- Reviewing by rereading rather than retrieving.
- Using rare synonyms without understanding register.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Basic reading comprehension
- Awareness of nouns, verbs and adjectives
- Dictionary use

### Follow-on candidates
- Academic vocabulary and technical terminology
- Reading comprehension and writing style
- Second-language fluency and lexical retrieval

### Diagnostic placement item
You recognise “analysis” but cannot use “analyse” or “analytical”. What dimensions of word knowledge are missing?

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Recognises translations but cannot retrieve or use words in sentences.
- **Developing:** Uses familiar words in prepared contexts; form/collocation errors remain.
- **Independent:** Retrieves and uses words with suitable grammar and collocation in varied contexts.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Infers likely meaning from morphology/context, verifies uncertainty and selects register-appropriate vocabulary independently.

### Retention and transfer
Memory-heavy but contextual: retrieve from meaning-to-form and form-to-use directions; space based on recall quality and mix old/new words in authentic output.

**Transfer task:** Read a short science or technology article and extract ten useful terms. Build word-family/collocation notes and write a plain-language explanation using them accurately.

## DEEP-LANG-003 — Grammar, sentence structure and editing

**Discipline:** Language & Linguistic Intelligence  
**Objective:** Analyze clause structure and use grammar to express relationships, emphasis and precise meaning.

### Conceptual model
Grammar describes patterns that organise words into phrases, clauses and sentences. Editing is not simply “make it sound formal”; it means preserve intended meaning while improving correctness, clarity, cohesion and fit for audience. A sentence can contain multiple clauses; each clause has a subject-predicate structure, and punctuation signals boundaries and relationships. Grammatical choices vary by language and register. Editing should proceed from meaning and structure to sentence-level correctness and finally surface polish.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** Repair a run-on: “I studied all night I was still confused”.
**Reasoning:** There are two independent clauses. Separate with a full stop, join with a semicolon, or use a conjunction with appropriate punctuation. Choose based on relationship.
**Result:** “I studied all night, but I was still confused.”

**Problem:** Improve “The experiment was done by us and it was good”.
**Reasoning:** Identify what “good” means and foreground useful information. If the result is known: “Our measurements were consistent across three trials.” This is more precise, not merely more formal.
**Result:** Replace vague evaluation with observable detail.

### Guided practice
- Mark subjects, verbs and clause boundaries in five sentences.
- Combine short related sentences using coordination or subordination without changing meaning.
- Edit a paragraph in passes: argument/meaning, structure, grammar, punctuation.

### Independent exercise bank
- Correct fragments, run-ons and comma splices in a short passage.
- Rewrite a vague paragraph for clarity while preserving all factual claims.
- Compare a formal and conversational version; explain register choices.
- Edit a Bangla or English paragraph while preserving language-specific grammar rather than translating word order mechanically.
- Explain why a sentence is ambiguous and produce two unambiguous alternatives.

### Common failure modes
- Editing words before understanding intended meaning.
- Using passive voice or complex vocabulary to sound academic without improving precision.
- Joining independent clauses with a comma alone.
- Applying English sentence patterns mechanically to Bangla or another language.
- Changing facts while polishing style.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Basic sentence comprehension
- Common parts of speech
- Reading and writing short paragraphs

### Follow-on candidates
- Academic writing and technical documentation
- Argumentative essays and reports
- Translation/editing across languages

### Diagnostic placement item
In “Because the circuit failed.”, what makes the sentence incomplete in standard written English, and how could it be repaired?

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Identifies some grammar errors but edits inconsistently or changes meaning.
- **Developing:** Repairs common sentence errors and improves clarity in short passages.
- **Independent:** Edits structure, grammar and punctuation while preserving meaning and audience fit.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Diagnoses ambiguity, adapts register across contexts/languages and explains consequential editorial choices.

### Retention and transfer
Generative language skill: edit authentic writing over time, then revisit after delay to detect recurring patterns. Use feedback on meaning and clarity, not only error counts.

**Transfer task:** Take a paragraph from a project explanation, edit it for a non-expert reader, then produce a technical version for a knowledgeable reader. Compare how grammar and vocabulary choices change.

## DEEP-COMM-001 — Audience, purpose and message design

**Discipline:** Communication & Rhetoric  
**Objective:** Adapt content, vocabulary, structure and detail to audience knowledge and communication purpose.

### Conceptual model
Communication is purposeful coordination of meaning between people. Effective message design begins with audience, desired outcome, context, constraints and channel. The same facts require different framing for a client, peer, teacher or technical collaborator. A useful message has a clear main point, relevant support, an explicit next step when needed and enough context to prevent misinterpretation. Concision is removal of unnecessary material, not removal of necessary reasoning.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** Explain a website bug to a client and to a developer.
**Reasoning:** Client version: describe user impact, current status and next update without implementation jargon. Developer version: provide reproduction steps, expected/actual behaviour, environment and relevant logs. Both must be truthful; only detail level changes.
**Result:** Audience-specific framing preserves facts while changing detail and vocabulary.

**Problem:** Ask a teacher for clarification about an unclear assignment.
**Reasoning:** State the exact instruction, explain the ambiguity, show your current interpretation and ask one answerable question. This reduces the burden on the recipient.
**Result:** A precise clarification request includes context and a bounded question.

### Guided practice
- Write a one-sentence main point before adding supporting details.
- Explain the same concept in 30 seconds to a younger learner and a technical peer.
- Turn a vague request into a message with context, desired action and deadline only if relevant.

### Independent exercise bank
- Create audience profiles for a client, collaborator and beginner user.
- Rewrite a long update into a concise status report without losing risks or next steps.
- Prepare a 60-second explanation with one analogy and one limitation.
- Choose a channel for urgent, sensitive and archival messages; justify trade-offs.
- Analyse a misunderstanding and identify missing context or mismatched assumptions.

### Common failure modes
- Starting with background before the main point.
- Using jargon to signal expertise rather than aid understanding.
- Being concise by omitting critical constraints.
- Assuming the recipient shares context they do not have.
- Failing to state what response or action is needed.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Basic written and spoken language
- Ability to identify purpose and key facts
- Listening to recipient needs

### Follow-on candidates
- Technical writing and documentation
- Negotiation and client communication
- Public speaking and leadership communication

### Diagnostic placement item
You need someone to test a broken feature. What information must they receive to reproduce it without asking you five follow-up questions?

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Message has relevant information but purpose/audience is unclear.
- **Developing:** Adapts tone and includes main point; context or next step may be missing.
- **Independent:** Designs clear audience-appropriate messages with sufficient context and actionable structure.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Handles high-context and cross-functional communication, anticipates misinterpretation and selects channel/detail strategically.

### Retention and transfer
Contextual performance: assess repeated messages across real audiences and channels. Include recipient comprehension/response as evidence, not self-confidence alone.

**Transfer task:** Prepare two versions of a project update: one for a client and one for a developer. Keep facts consistent while changing terminology, detail and requested action.

## DEEP-COMM-002 — Argument structure and evidence-based persuasion

**Discipline:** Communication & Rhetoric  
**Objective:** Construct claims supported by reasons and evidence; represent counterarguments fairly.

### Conceptual model
An argument is a structured case for a conclusion. A claim states what should be accepted; reasons explain why; evidence supports reasons; warrants connect evidence to the claim; qualifications and rebuttals show where the case is limited or contested. Persuasion is not volume or confidence. Strong argument distinguishes facts, values and assumptions, represents opposing positions fairly and changes strength when evidence changes. Evidence quality and relevance matter more than quantity.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** Claim: “Students should learn basic programming.” Build a bounded argument.
**Reasoning:** Reason: programming develops procedural problem-solving and enables digital creation. Evidence would need to show learning outcomes, not just job-market slogans. Warrant: these capabilities are useful across disciplines. Qualification: depth and timing should fit learner goals and access; programming is not the only route to reasoning.
**Result:** A defensible argument includes reasons, relevant evidence and a qualification.

**Problem:** Spot a weak argument: “This app is best because millions use it.”
**Reasoning:** Popularity may be evidence of adoption, not quality for a particular need. Ask what “best” means, compare relevant criteria, and seek performance/usability evidence.
**Result:** Popularity alone does not establish suitability.

### Guided practice
- Separate claim, reason, evidence and assumption in a short editorial.
- Construct a claim-evidence-warrant chain from a small dataset.
- Write the strongest fair objection to your own claim before replying.

### Independent exercise bank
- Write a 300-word argument with at least two evidence types and one limitation.
- Identify a correlation-to-causation leap in a supplied argument.
- Compare two options using explicit criteria and explain weighting.
- Steelman an opposing view before rebutting it.
- Revise a claim after receiving counter-evidence; document what changed.

### Common failure modes
- Treating assertion or anecdote as sufficient evidence.
- Straw-manning opposing views.
- Cherry-picking data and ignoring base rates.
- Confusing popularity, authority or confidence with truth.
- Refusing to revise a claim after contrary evidence.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Audience and purpose design
- Claim/evidence distinction
- Basic source evaluation

### Follow-on candidates
- Debate and negotiation
- Policy analysis and decision memos
- Critical reasoning and fallacy analysis

### Diagnostic placement item
“Everyone in my group agrees, so the idea is correct.” Identify the reasoning error and what evidence would be more relevant.

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** States a position but reasons/evidence are weak or disconnected.
- **Developing:** Provides reasons and examples but warrants, counterarguments or limitations are incomplete.
- **Independent:** Builds a coherent claim-evidence-warrant structure, addresses counterarguments and qualifies conclusions.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Evaluates competing arguments, calibrates claims to evidence and revises position transparently when warranted.

### Retention and transfer
Argumentative skill: alternate written and spoken cases, include opposing evidence and delayed revision. Reward fair representation and calibration, not winning or rhetorical aggression.

**Transfer task:** Write a recommendation memo for a real low-stakes choice. Define criteria, compare options, cite evidence, state trade-offs and identify what new information would change your recommendation.

## DEEP-COMM-003 — Listening, questioning and dialogue

**Discipline:** Communication & Rhetoric  
**Objective:** Listen for meaning, reflect accurately, ask clarifying questions and respond without premature assumptions.

### Conceptual model
Listening is active information processing, not waiting for a turn to speak. It involves attention, interpretation, checking meaning and responding appropriately. Open questions invite explanation; closed questions confirm specific facts. Clarifying questions reduce ambiguity, while leading questions can distort answers. Paraphrasing is a test of understanding, not a claim that the listener agrees. Good dialogue makes disagreement discussable by separating the person from the proposition and checking assumptions.

### Worked examples
**Problem:** A teammate says, “The project is going badly.” Respond productively.
**Reasoning:** Avoid immediate advice. Ask an open question (“Which part is causing the most trouble?”), listen, paraphrase (“So the deadline is at risk because the image assets are missing?”), then confirm before proposing options.
**Result:** Clarify the problem before solving it.

**Problem:** Turn “You never explain things properly” into a constructive clarification.
**Reasoning:** Describe the specific point that is unclear and ask for the missing step: “I understand the first calculation, but not why the denominator changes here. Could you show that step?”
**Result:** Specific observation + bounded question.

### Guided practice
- After a short explanation, paraphrase the main point and ask whether it is accurate.
- Convert three leading questions into neutral open questions.
- Practise a two-minute conversation where the goal is to understand, not persuade.

### Independent exercise bank
- Conduct a short interview with consent and summarise the person’s view without adding assumptions.
- Use clarifying, probing and reflective questions in a problem-solving conversation.
- Identify a moment when you interrupted or prepared a reply instead of listening; plan a corrective behaviour.
- Disagree with a claim while accurately restating the strongest version first.
- Ask for feedback and paraphrase it before explaining your response.

### Common failure modes
- Offering advice before understanding the issue.
- Using questions as disguised accusations.
- Paraphrasing inaccurately and then defending the paraphrase.
- Confusing listening with agreement.
- Interrupting or filling silence too quickly.

### Prerequisite candidates
- Basic conversational language
- Audience and purpose awareness
- Respectful turn-taking

### Follow-on candidates
- Negotiation and conflict resolution
- Interviewing and user research
- Leadership, coaching and facilitation

### Diagnostic placement item
Someone says “I’m not sure this plan will work.” What is one open question and one paraphrase that would clarify their concern?

### Assessment rubric
- **Emerging:** Hears surface content but misses key concerns or interrupts frequently.
- **Developing:** Uses questions and paraphrase with prompting; occasionally assumes meaning.
- **Independent:** Listens without premature judgement, clarifies, accurately paraphrases and responds to the actual concern.
- **Transfer_Ready:** Facilitates disagreement, detects ambiguity and adapts questioning to emotional/contextual stakes while respecting boundaries.

### Retention and transfer
Interpersonal performance: use real conversations and feedback over time. Evidence includes accurate summaries and better problem definition; do not grade personality or extroversion.

**Transfer task:** In a project discussion, ask a teammate to explain a concern. Paraphrase it, confirm accuracy, ask one probing question and only then suggest a next step.
