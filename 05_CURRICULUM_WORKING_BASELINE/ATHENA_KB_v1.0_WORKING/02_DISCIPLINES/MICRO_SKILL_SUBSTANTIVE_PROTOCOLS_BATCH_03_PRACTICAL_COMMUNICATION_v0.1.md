**Scope:** 20 practical-independence and communication protocols. This batch replaces compact catalogue entries for the listed IDs when quality is assessed.


## MS-03-001 — Fold a T-shirt consistently

**Target performance:** Produce a compact, repeatable fold without stretching seams or trapping bulky wrinkles.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a clean, dry shirt on a clear flat surface; compare shirts of similar size and fabric first.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Lay the shirt front-down and smooth it gently from shoulders to hem. 2. Fold one side inward so its edge approaches the centre line; fold the sleeve back so it lies inside the new rectangle. 3. Repeat on the other side, keeping the body width similar. 4. Fold the lower third upward, then fold again to the desired storage size. 5. Turn it over and check that the collar, hem and side edges are aligned. 6. Unfold and inspect for twisted sleeves or stretched fabric; correct the step that caused the distortion. 7. Repeat from the start without looking at the sequence.

**Practice dose:** Practise 3–5 shirts in one session, varying only one factor at a time (fabric thickness or size). Stop if fabric is damp or delicate.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Fold five mixed shirts. At least four should form stable, similarly sized rectangles, with sleeves contained and no severe twisting; record approximate final width and height.

**Common failure modes:** Pulling fabric hard; folding sleeves inconsistently; changing the method on every repetition; prioritising tiny size over fabric care.

**Delayed retention check:** After 48 hours, fold two shirts without prompts and compare alignment and dimensions with the first session.

**Transfer task:** Teach another person the sequence, then adapt it for a thick hoodie or a small child-sized shirt without forcing the same dimensions.

**Safety and scope:** Ordinary household task. Use clean garments and a stable surface; do not iron or use heat as part of this protocol.

**Evidence to record:** Before/after photos, garment type, dimensions, prompt use and delayed unaided result.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-03-002 — Pack a small bag by checklist

**Target performance:** Pack the items required for a defined short outing while preventing omissions, damage and unnecessary weight.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Choose a real low-risk outing and its duration, weather and activities. List required items before opening the bag.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Write the destination, duration and likely conditions. 2. Divide items into essential, conditional and optional. 3. Check each item against the actual task rather than habit. 4. Place heavy items close to the back panel and items needed soon near the top or an accessible pocket. 5. Protect electronics and papers from liquids; separate dirty or wet items. 6. Check capacity, closures and weight before leaving. 7. Read the checklist once while physically touching each item, then remove nonessential duplicates. 8. After the outing, note what was unused, missing or difficult to reach.

**Practice dose:** Complete one pack-and-review cycle per outing; use different checklists for school, sport and day trips rather than one universal list.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** For three outings, carry all essentials, avoid preventable damage and identify at least one item that can be removed or repositioned. The bag must remain comfortable and close securely.

**Common failure modes:** Packing from memory; adding “just in case” objects without a reason; putting liquids beside electronics; placing frequently used items at the bottom.

**Delayed retention check:** At the next comparable outing, pack from a revised checklist without relying on the previous packed bag.

**Transfer task:** Create a reusable checklist for a different outing and explain which items are conditional on weather, duration or activity.

**Safety and scope:** Do not pack medicines or safety equipment contrary to guardian/professional instructions. Keep the load appropriate for the learner; do not test carrying limits.

**Evidence to record:** Checklist version, omitted/unused items, damage or access issues, comfort note and delayed packing result.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-03-003 — Sew on a button

**Target performance:** Attach a loose button securely with neat thread tension while avoiding puckering or needle injury.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a spare button, scrap fabric or an adult-approved garment repair. Use a blunt practice needle first if needed; work in good light.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Match button size and hole pattern to the garment. 2. Thread a suitable needle and tie a secure knot; avoid excessive thread length. 3. Mark the button position and align it with the buttonhole. 4. Bring the needle from the reverse side through one hole, then through the paired hole; repeat evenly. 5. Keep the thread snug but leave enough clearance for fabric thickness and buttonhole use. 6. For a shank-style attachment, create a small thread shank rather than crushing the fabric. 7. Finish on the reverse side with several secure locking stitches and trim thread safely. 8. Test fastening gently and inspect for puckering.

**Practice dose:** Practise on scrap fabric for 10–15 minutes, then attempt one real repair only when the practice stitches are controlled.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Button remains aligned after 10 gentle fastening cycles; thread is secure, fabric is not puckered and the buttonhole can pass over the button without excessive force.

**Common failure modes:** Pulling every stitch too tight; using thread too long; sewing through the wrong holes; leaving loose tails; choosing a button incompatible with the buttonhole.

**Delayed retention check:** Inspect after several days and repeat the gentle fastening test; repair loose stitches before the button detaches.

**Transfer task:** Explain the difference between a flat four-hole button and a shank button, then select an appropriate stitch path for each on scrap fabric.

**Safety and scope:** Needle is sharp. Work seated, keep fingers out of the needle path, store needle immediately after use and ask for adult help if unfamiliar. Do not sew while distracted.

**Evidence to record:** Fabric/button type, stitch pattern, fastening test, repair photo and delayed inspection.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-03-004 — Tie shoelaces securely

**Target performance:** Tie a stable bow that stays closed during ordinary walking and can be untied deliberately.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use shoes that fit and laces in good condition; practise seated before walking.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Cross the lace ends and pass one under the other; pull until the shoe is snug but not constricting. 2. Form a loop with one lace. 3. Wrap the other lace around the loop base. 4. Push a bight through the opening to form the second loop. 5. Hold both loops and pull evenly until the knot sits flat. 6. Check that loops are similar and ends are not excessively long. 7. Walk briefly on a clear surface and check whether the knot loosens. 8. If it slips, inspect lace material and knot orientation; learn a double bow only if appropriate.

**Practice dose:** Practise five slow ties while seated, then two ties without looking at the steps. Avoid rushing.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Complete three consecutive ties that remain secure through a short supervised walk, with no painful pressure or trailing lace hazard.

**Common failure modes:** Pulling only the ends rather than loops; creating an uneven knot; tying too tightly; ignoring worn or slippery laces.

**Delayed retention check:** After 2–3 days, tie independently and check after a short walk.

**Transfer task:** Tie a different pair of shoes and explain how lace length and material affect the result.

**Safety and scope:** Loose laces can cause trips. Practise seated, keep the floor clear and do not run until the knot is checked.

**Evidence to record:** Unaided tie attempts, knot stability after walk, comfort and delayed performance.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-03-005 — Read a measuring tape

**Target performance:** Read metric length accurately, including the zero point, millimetres and appropriate precision.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a metric tape with legible markings and a flat, nonhazardous object. Avoid cutting or construction work.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Identify the zero mark; do not assume the metal hook edge is the zero reference without checking its movement design. 2. Place the tape straight along the dimension and keep it aligned with the object edge. 3. Read the centimetre number before the endpoint. 4. Count millimetre divisions after that number; ten millimetres equal one centimetre. 5. Record units with every measurement. 6. Repeat from a fresh placement and compare readings. 7. For a curved object, do not force a rigid tape; choose a suitable flexible measure. 8. State the measurement precision justified by the scale.

**Practice dose:** Measure five household objects twice each. Include one object near a centimetre boundary and one longer than 30 cm.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** At least 9 of 10 readings agree within 2 mm, with correct units and no parallax or zero-offset error.

**Common failure modes:** Starting at the tape casing; reading centimetres as millimetres; bending the tape; reporting more precision than markings support.

**Delayed retention check:** Repeat three measurements after 48 hours and explain any discrepancy greater than 2 mm.

**Transfer task:** Measure a room object or package and communicate the dimensions clearly to someone else; include uncertainty where edges are irregular.

**Safety and scope:** Keep tape away from face and moving machinery. Do not use a measuring tape as a toy or as a load-bearing tool.

**Evidence to record:** Object, repeated readings, tape resolution, difference between repeats and explanation.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-03-006 — Use a spirit level

**Target performance:** Determine whether a surface is approximately horizontal or vertical and identify the direction of deviation.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use an undamaged small level on a stable surface; do not climb or work near edges.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Inspect the level body and vial for cracks or fluid leakage. 2. Place it on the surface in the intended orientation. 3. Let it settle and observe the bubble relative to the marked centre lines. 4. Record which side the bubble moves toward; follow the instrument’s markings rather than guessing. 5. Rotate the level 180 degrees on the same surface and compare the reading to detect possible instrument error. 6. Repeat at a second position. 7. For vertical checks, place the level against a safe upright face. 8. Report “approximately level” rather than claiming perfect geometry from a coarse vial.

**Practice dose:** Use three safe, low-height surfaces. Perform two orientations per surface and record results.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Correctly identify level/tilt direction in at least five of six checks and notice a clearly inconsistent reversed reading.

**Common failure modes:** Reading the bubble backwards; checking only one position; assuming the surface is level because the bubble is near centre; using a damaged vial.

**Delayed retention check:** Repeat on a known flat reference after several days and compare orientation results.

**Transfer task:** Explain why rotating the level helps detect instrument bias and why a spirit level does not measure exact angle without calibration.

**Safety and scope:** No ladders, roof edges, heavy objects or powered tools. Adult supervision for any installation task.

**Evidence to record:** Surface, orientation, bubble position, repeatability and any instrument concern.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-03-007 — Basic screwdriver selection

**Target performance:** Select a screwdriver tip that fits a fastener head and apply controlled hand force without damaging it.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use an unplugged, nonpowered practice board or loose large fastener. Do not work on electrical equipment.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Inspect the fastener head and identify its drive pattern: slotted, Phillips, Pozidriv, hex or another type. 2. Select a tip that matches both pattern and size. 3. Seat the tip fully; it should not rock or contact only the edges. 4. Hold the handle in line with the screw axis. 5. Apply moderate axial pressure and turn slowly. 6. Stop if the tip cams out, the head deforms or resistance rises unexpectedly. 7. Try a different correct size rather than increasing force. 8. Return the screw to its original position without overtightening.

**Practice dose:** Compare at least four tip types using a practice fastener. One short session; prioritise fit over speed.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Correctly match at least 4 of 5 fasteners and complete controlled turns without visible head damage or slipping.

**Common failure modes:** Using a near-fit tip; tilting the driver; pressing too lightly or excessively; using a screwdriver as a pry tool.

**Delayed retention check:** After a week, identify five unfamiliar fastener heads from a safe sample and select the correct tool before turning.

**Transfer task:** Explain how a mismatched tip damages the drive recess and why powered drivers require additional torque control.

**Safety and scope:** Never use on live electrical equipment, swollen batteries or unknown devices. Keep fingers clear of the tip and use adult supervision for repairs.

**Evidence to record:** Fastener type, selected tip, fit quality, slippage/damage and delayed identification.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-03-008 — Organise a digital folder

**Target performance:** Create a predictable folder structure and file names that make project files easy to locate without duplicating or losing originals.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a practice folder containing copies, not irreplaceable personal files. Choose a simple project with 6–12 files.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Define the project and the main retrieval questions (“source”, “draft”, “final”, “assets”). 2. Create a root folder with a clear project name. 3. Create only the subfolders needed for distinct file roles. 4. Rename copies using a consistent pattern such as YYYY-MM-DD_topic_version; avoid ambiguous names like final-final. 5. Keep source files separate from exports and working drafts. 6. Move files in small groups and verify each destination. 7. Search for one file by name and one by folder path. 8. Remove empty duplicates only after confirming that a verified copy exists.

**Practice dose:** Organise one small practice project in 20–30 minutes. Do not reorganise the entire device in one pass.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** A second person can locate five requested files within 30 seconds each; no original is lost and naming conventions are consistent.

**Common failure modes:** Over-nesting folders; vague filenames; moving originals before verifying copies; creating several competing “final” versions.

**Delayed retention check:** After one week, retrieve the same five files without relying on memory of the exact path; update structure only if retrieval fails.

**Transfer task:** Apply the scheme to a different project and explain which folders are reusable versus project-specific.

**Safety and scope:** Do not expose private documents or credentials in shared folders. Back up before bulk moves or deletion.

**Evidence to record:** Before/after tree, naming rule, retrieval times, duplicates found and delayed retrieval results.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-03-009 — Make a simple spreadsheet table

**Target performance:** Build a structured table with one record per row, consistent fields and basic sorting/filtering.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a spreadsheet app and fictional or non-sensitive sample data with 8–15 records.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Decide the question the table should answer. 2. Define one header per field and use consistent units and formats. 3. Enter one record per row; do not merge cells inside the data region. 4. Keep each column to one data type where practical. 5. Format dates and numbers consistently. 6. Convert the range to a table or enable filters. 7. Sort one field and check that entire rows move together. 8. Filter for a condition and verify the visible records manually. 9. Add a simple total only when the field is numeric and the total is meaningful.

**Practice dose:** Create one table with at least 10 records and three to five columns. Repeat sorting and filtering with different fields.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** No blank or duplicate headers; records remain intact after sorting; filters return the expected subset; totals can be checked manually.

**Common failure modes:** Putting multiple facts in one cell; sorting only one column; using colour as the only data meaning; mixing units or text formats.

**Delayed retention check:** After several days, recreate a smaller table from a blank sheet and correctly filter a new condition.

**Transfer task:** Explain when a spreadsheet table is insufficient and a database with validation/relationships would be more appropriate.

**Safety and scope:** Use fictional data for practice; avoid uploading personal information to untrusted services.

**Evidence to record:** Sample dataset, field definitions, filter query, expected/actual result and delayed recreation.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-03-010 — Read a public transport route

**Target performance:** Plan a route using official route information, direction, interchange and service conditions.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a current official map or operator source for a familiar route; practise planning before travelling.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Identify origin and destination precisely, including which entrance or stop matters. 2. Locate the correct line/route and direction, not merely the route number. 3. Count stops and identify interchange points. 4. Check service hours, disruptions, fare and accessibility information from the operator. 5. Compare a direct route with an interchange route using total time and transfer risk. 6. Note the stop where you must exit and what landmark confirms it. 7. Prepare a fallback if a service is unavailable. 8. Before any real trip, confirm live service information and tell a trusted adult your plan when appropriate.

**Practice dose:** Plan two routes on paper or map app; do not travel alone solely to practise.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** For two scenarios, identify correct direction, boarding point, exit point and at least one viable fallback with no critical interchange error.

**Common failure modes:** Following a line in the wrong direction; confusing station names; relying on stale screenshots; ignoring last-service times or walking distance.

**Delayed retention check:** After a week, plan a different route from a blank start and verify all current service details.

**Transfer task:** Explain the trade-off between fewer transfers, shorter journey time, reliability and accessibility.

**Safety and scope:** Real travel must follow guardian guidance and local safety practices. Stay in public areas, keep belongings secure and do not use unsafe shortcuts.

**Evidence to record:** Source and access date, planned stops, transfer logic, fallback and later route-planning result.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-04-001 — Introduce yourself clearly

**Target performance:** Deliver a concise, context-appropriate introduction that communicates relevant identity, purpose and a natural next conversational step.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Choose a low-pressure context such as a class group or supervised club. Decide what information is appropriate to share.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Identify the audience and reason for introducing yourself. 2. Select only relevant details: name or preferred form of address, current role/context, and one genuine interest or purpose. 3. Build a 15–25 second version with a clear opening and closing prompt. 4. Practise at a comfortable pace and audible volume. 5. Deliver while looking toward the listener naturally; do not force eye contact. 6. Pause for the other person’s response. 7. Adapt the introduction to a formal and informal context without inventing achievements.

**Practice dose:** Practise twice alone, then use it in one real low-stakes interaction. Avoid rehearsing until it sounds scripted.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Listener can recall the main context and one relevant detail; introduction fits within 15–30 seconds and invites a response without oversharing.

**Common failure modes:** Listing irrelevant facts; speaking too quickly; using exaggerated claims; turning the introduction into a monologue.

**Delayed retention check:** After several days, introduce yourself in a different setting with no written script.

**Transfer task:** Create a shorter version for a group and a more formal version for a project meeting; preserve truth while changing emphasis.

**Safety and scope:** Do not disclose address, school schedule, passwords or other sensitive personal details to strangers.

**Evidence to record:** Context, approximate duration, listener recall, comfort note and second-context performance.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-04-002 — Ask a precise question

**Target performance:** Form a question that makes the information gap, context and desired level of answer clear.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a real learning or project problem that is not urgent or safety-critical. Attempt to identify what is already known.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. State the task or claim you are trying to understand. 2. Write what you already know and where you are stuck. 3. Identify the smallest unresolved point. 4. Ask one question at a time, including relevant context or an example. 5. Avoid embedding an untested assumption in the wording. 6. If the answer is broad, ask a targeted follow-up. 7. Restate the answer in your own words to check understanding.

**Practice dose:** Rewrite five vague questions into precise ones; use one in a real learning interaction.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** A knowledgeable listener can answer without first asking what the question refers to; the response addresses the intended uncertainty.

**Common failure modes:** Asking several unrelated questions at once; omitting context; asking “why doesn’t this work?” without showing expected and actual behaviour.

**Delayed retention check:** After 48 hours, write three precise questions from a new topic and check whether each exposes one answerable gap.

**Transfer task:** Use the method in a bug report, classroom question and research query; adapt detail to audience.

**Safety and scope:** Do not include passwords, personal records or confidential information when seeking help.

**Evidence to record:** Original and revised questions, context included, follow-up and whether the answer resolved the gap.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-04-003 — Active listening summary

**Target performance:** Accurately paraphrase another person’s main point, reasons and unresolved concern before responding.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Practise with a willing person discussing a non-sensitive topic; agree that this is a listening exercise.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Let the speaker finish without interrupting. 2. Note the central claim and two supporting details mentally or briefly. 3. Separate what they said from your interpretation. 4. Summarise using neutral wording: “You’re saying…, mainly because…”. 5. Ask whether the summary is accurate. 6. Invite correction and revise it without defensiveness. 7. Only then offer your response or question.

**Practice dose:** Use one 3–5 minute conversation, then switch roles. Do not turn every conversation into a formal exercise.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Speaker confirms that the summary captures the main point and does not distort their position; corrections are incorporated.

**Common failure modes:** Planning a rebuttal while the other person speaks; parroting words without understanding; adding motives they did not state.

**Delayed retention check:** Repeat with a different speaker after several days and compare the first summary with the speaker’s correction.

**Transfer task:** Use in a team disagreement and write a neutral summary of both positions before proposing a solution.

**Safety and scope:** Respect privacy; do not record conversations without consent. Do not use the technique to pressure someone into disclosure.

**Evidence to record:** Speaker-confirmed summary, correction count, omitted point and delayed conversation reflection.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-04-004 — Give specific feedback

**Target performance:** Give feedback tied to observable behaviour, its effect and a feasible next step without attacking identity.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Choose a low-stakes work sample or practice performance. Ensure feedback is invited or appropriate to the relationship.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Describe the task and standard being used. 2. Identify one observable behaviour, quoting or pointing to a specific moment. 3. Explain its effect on the result or audience. 4. Name what worked, if relevant and genuine. 5. Offer one actionable next step or ask what support is wanted. 6. Avoid global labels such as “lazy” or “talentless”. 7. Invite the recipient to check whether the feedback is useful.

**Practice dose:** Practise on a sample paragraph, presentation or project artefact before giving personal feedback.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Recipient can identify the exact behaviour and one next action; feedback contains no unsupported character judgement.

**Common failure modes:** Vague praise; personal attacks; overwhelming someone with ten corrections; presenting preference as universal rule.

**Delayed retention check:** After a week, review whether the suggested action was attempted and whether it improved the work.

**Transfer task:** Adapt feedback for peer collaboration and formal review; distinguish objective defects from stylistic preference.

**Safety and scope:** Give feedback privately where appropriate and avoid humiliating or coercive delivery.

**Evidence to record:** Work sample, criterion, specific observation, next action and recipient clarification.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-04-005 — Disagree respectfully

**Target performance:** Challenge a claim or proposal while accurately representing it and preserving the possibility of constructive discussion.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a low-stakes disagreement where both people can safely opt out. Identify the exact proposition in dispute.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Restate the other position in a way its speaker would recognise. 2. Identify the specific point of disagreement. 3. State your own position with calibrated language. 4. Give one relevant reason or piece of evidence. 5. Ask what evidence or condition might change either view. 6. Listen to the response and update if warranted. 7. If the discussion becomes personal or unproductive, pause and return later.

**Practice dose:** Practise one structured disagreement using a neutral topic; do not manufacture conflict.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Both positions are represented fairly; the disagreement is specific; one reason is supplied; no insult or straw man is used.

**Common failure modes:** Using sarcasm; changing the subject; attributing motives; treating concession as defeat; escalating after a boundary is set.

**Delayed retention check:** After several days, write a fair two-sentence summary of a different opposing view before giving your response.

**Transfer task:** Apply to a project decision by listing shared goals, disputed assumptions and a test that could resolve the issue.

**Safety and scope:** Do not use the protocol to confront unsafe people or override boundaries. Seek trusted adult help for bullying, threats or coercion.

**Evidence to record:** Position summaries, evidence cited, any revision and whether the conversation stayed constructive.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-04-006 — Speak for one minute without notes

**Target performance:** Deliver a coherent one-minute explanation with a clear structure and controlled pace without reading a script.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Choose a familiar, low-stakes topic. Prepare three keywords only, then remove them for the test.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Define one central message. 2. Select a three-part structure: point, explanation/example, takeaway. 3. Practise once using three keywords. 4. Speak for 45–75 seconds without reading. 5. Pause between sections and use a concrete example. 6. Record audio if useful and listen for unclear transitions, filler overload and rushed endings. 7. Repeat once with one targeted improvement.

**Practice dose:** Two attempts per session are enough; avoid repeated takes that cause vocal strain.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Listener can state the main message and one supporting example; delivery falls within 45–75 seconds and contains no major factual error.

**Common failure modes:** Memorising every sentence; filling every pause; adding unrelated points to reach a minute; speaking beyond comfortable volume.

**Delayed retention check:** After 3–7 days, speak on a new familiar topic with no notes and compare structure.

**Transfer task:** Adapt the same explanation for a younger learner and a project teammate without changing the facts.

**Safety and scope:** Use comfortable speaking volume and stop if throat pain, dizziness or strain occurs.

**Evidence to record:** Topic, duration, outline remembered, listener recall and one improvement target.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-04-007 — Summarise a long message

**Target performance:** Extract the purpose, key facts, decisions and requested actions from a long message without changing its meaning.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a public or non-sensitive message of 300–800 words; preserve the original for verification.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Read once to identify the overall purpose. 2. Read again and mark facts, decisions, requests, deadlines and uncertainties separately. 3. Remove examples and repetition that do not change the decision. 4. Draft a summary in your own words, retaining names/dates only when necessary. 5. Create an action list with owner and deadline only if the source states them. 6. Compare each sentence against the original. 7. Mark missing or ambiguous information rather than inventing it.

**Practice dose:** Summarise one message in 5–10 minutes; practise with different formats such as announcement, project update and instructions.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** A reader can identify the purpose, all critical actions and deadlines; no unsupported owner, date or decision is introduced.

**Common failure modes:** Overcompressing away a condition; confusing proposal with decision; inventing deadlines; copying large passages verbatim.

**Delayed retention check:** After 48 hours, reconstruct the summary from the original without viewing the first summary and compare omissions.

**Transfer task:** Create a concise handoff summary for a project task and include explicit unknowns.

**Safety and scope:** Remove personal or confidential information before sharing summaries outside the intended audience.

**Evidence to record:** Original length, summary, critical-fact checklist, unsupported additions check and delayed comparison.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-04-008 — Read aloud with punctuation

**Target performance:** Read a short passage with phrasing that reflects syntax and punctuation while maintaining intelligibility and comfortable pace.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Choose a passage at an appropriate reading level, 100–180 words. Mark sentence boundaries and clauses.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Read silently to understand meaning. 2. Mark full stops, commas, questions and clause boundaries. 3. Read slowly, allowing phrase boundaries without inserting a pause at every comma mechanically. 4. Record a short sample. 5. Listen for omitted endings, swallowed consonants, rushed clauses and unnatural pauses. 6. Repeat with attention to one issue. 7. Read a fresh passage to test transfer rather than memorising the first.

**Practice dose:** Practise 5–8 minutes. Stop if voice becomes tired; use comfortable pitch and volume.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** A listener understands the passage; sentence boundaries are clear; punctuation informs phrasing without exaggerated pauses; key word endings remain audible.

**Common failure modes:** Reading word-by-word; treating every comma as an equal pause; raising volume instead of articulating; racing through sentence endings.

**Delayed retention check:** After several days, read a new passage and ask a listener to identify the main idea.

**Transfer task:** Apply to a short presentation or instructions, adjusting pace for audience comprehension.

**Safety and scope:** No shouting or forced accent imitation. Stop if throat pain or persistent hoarseness occurs.

**Evidence to record:** Passage, recording, listener comprehension, pacing notes and fresh-passage result.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-04-009 — Negotiate a simple agreement

**Target performance:** Reach a clear, voluntary agreement by identifying interests, options, constraints and next actions.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a low-stakes scenario with a willing peer or family member, such as dividing a shared task. Avoid money, legal or high-stakes matters.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Define the decision and what each person needs. 2. Let each person state their priorities and constraints. 3. Separate fixed requirements from preferences. 4. Generate at least two possible arrangements before choosing. 5. Compare options against fairness, feasibility and consequences. 6. State the agreed action, who will do it and by when. 7. Ask each person to confirm the wording freely. 8. Set a check-in or revision condition if circumstances change.

**Practice dose:** One 5–10 minute scenario; switch roles in a second scenario. Do not practise by pressuring someone.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Both participants can independently restate the agreement; actions and timing are clear; either person can decline or propose revision.

**Common failure modes:** Treating negotiation as winning; hiding constraints; vague commitments; assuming silence means agreement; applying pressure.

**Delayed retention check:** Check after the agreed time whether the arrangement was followed and whether the terms were workable.

**Transfer task:** Use the method for a group project task split and document responsibilities and a fair revision path.

**Safety and scope:** Agreement must be voluntary. For disputes involving safety, coercion, money or legal obligations, involve a trusted adult or qualified professional.

**Evidence to record:** Initial interests, options considered, final terms, participant confirmation and outcome.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.


## MS-04-010 — Write a concise professional message

**Target performance:** Write a short message that makes its purpose, context and requested action clear while using an appropriate tone.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a fictional or low-risk scenario. Identify recipient role, channel and desired outcome.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Write the purpose in one sentence. 2. Add only context the recipient needs to act. 3. State the request or next action explicitly. 4. Include a deadline only when real and relevant. 5. Use a neutral greeting and closing suited to the relationship. 6. Remove repeated background, emotional filler and ambiguous pronouns. 7. Check names, dates, attachments and tone before sending. 8. Read it from the recipient’s perspective: can they tell what to do next?

**Practice dose:** Draft three messages for different contexts (request, update, clarification), each under 120 words unless complexity requires more.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Recipient can identify purpose and next action on first reading; no invented urgency; spelling, names and dates are correct.

**Common failure modes:** Overly formal language for a familiar recipient; vague “let me know”; missing context; passive-aggressive phrasing; unnecessary personal data.

**Delayed retention check:** After a few days, rewrite a new message from a blank page and check whether it remains concise and complete.

**Transfer task:** Adapt the same request for email, team chat and a formal school context; preserve facts while changing conventions.

**Safety and scope:** Do not send private credentials, personal documents or sensitive details to an unverified recipient.

**Evidence to record:** Draft, recipient/context, word count, action clarity check and delayed independent draft.

**Status:** Draft authored; independent subject review, learner testing and calibration required.
