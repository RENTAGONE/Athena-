# ATHENA Substantive Micro-skill Protocols — Legacy Batch 02

**Status:** Substantive authored drafts; independent review and learner testing required. Not certifications.

**Scope:** Ten legacy micro-skills with distinct cognitive, observational, memory, sleep, reading, auditory, dexterity and ethical boundaries.

## MS-15-001 — Doomsday Algorithm — weekday mental calculation

**Target performance:** Given a Gregorian calendar date, calculate its weekday using a named anchor method and show intermediate values so arithmetic or century-rule errors can be located.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use the Gregorian calendar only. Learn one published Doomsday method from a reliable mathematics reference; do not mix month codes from different methods. Begin with dates in a familiar non-leap year.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Memorise the seven-day cycle and the century anchor for the selected method. 2. Learn the year-anchor calculation in separate parts: century contribution, last two digits, floor division by four, and final modulo-seven reduction. 3. Learn month anchor dates for ordinary years. 4. Apply the leap-year adjustment only for January and February in leap years. 5. Work each date on paper, writing every intermediate value and reducing modulo seven at the end. 6. Check answers against an independent calendar. 7. Practise century boundaries and leap-year exceptions only after ordinary cases are reliable. 8. When an answer is wrong, identify whether the error came from the anchor, year arithmetic, month code, leap-year adjustment or weekday mapping.

**Practice dose:** 10–15 dates per short session, split by difficulty. Do not practise speed until accuracy is stable; use mixed dates rather than repeating one month.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** For 20 unseen dates spanning ordinary years, leap years, January/February and century exceptions, achieve at least 18 correct and show a valid calculation path for every answer. A correct guess without traceable reasoning is not sufficient.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: forgetting the Gregorian century exception; applying leap adjustment to all months; mixing Sunday-first and Monday-first numbering; arithmetic mistakes; and assuming the method applies to non-Gregorian calendars.

**Delayed retention check:** Repeat 10 unseen dates after 3–7 days without reviewing month codes immediately beforehand. Relearn the specific component responsible for any error.

**Transfer task:** Use the method to verify a historical date or plan a future event, then cross-check with a trusted calendar. Explain why a different calendar system may yield a different weekday.

**Safety and scope:** This is a low-risk mental arithmetic skill. Do not treat calendar calculation as evidence about historical date accuracy; calendar reforms and local adoption dates can matter.

**Evidence to record:** Written intermediate calculations, date set, answer key source, error classification, cold delayed test.

## MS-15-002 — Visual Observation — structured scene description

**Target performance:** Describe a visible scene using specific, neutral observations while separating directly visible details from inference and uncertainty.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Choose a safe, ordinary scene or still-life image. Avoid photographing or profiling strangers, private spaces, identifying documents or sensitive personal attributes.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Observe silently for 20–30 seconds before writing. 2. Record the overall setting and major objects without guessing purpose or identity. 3. Describe spatial relations (left/right, foreground/background, near/far) and visible actions. 4. Add measurable or countable details where possible: number, colour, shape, orientation, visible text. 5. Separate direct observations from interpretations in different columns. 6. Mark uncertain details as uncertain rather than filling gaps. 7. Recheck the scene and correct omissions or contradictions. 8. Give another person the description and ask which details they can locate without additional inference.

**Practice dose:** Use one scene for 5–8 minutes. Practise with varied scenes; avoid turning observation into prolonged surveillance or intrusive attention to people.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** A reader can identify the intended scene or reproduce its major layout from the description; at least 90% of listed details are visible and supportable; interpretations are labelled separately; no sensitive identity inference is included.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: stating inferred motives as facts; overlooking spatial relationships; using vague adjectives; adding details that are not visible; and focusing only on salient objects while missing context.

**Delayed retention check:** Describe a new scene after several days, then compare the description with the image. Track factual additions and missed relationships.

**Transfer task:** Use the method when inspecting a diagram, photograph or experiment setup. Transfer succeeds when observation remains separate from explanation.

**Safety and scope:** Privacy-first: do not record or infer sensitive traits, identities, addresses, routines or vulnerabilities of real people. Use consented or non-personal scenes.

**Evidence to record:** Scene description, observation/inference split, uncertainty notes, reviewer reconstruction and delayed sample.

**Status:** Draft authored; review requirements remain open.

## MS-15-003 — Memory Palace — method of loci

**Target performance:** Use a familiar, stable route to encode and retrieve an ordered set of information, while verifying recall rather than relying on vividness alone.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Choose a familiar indoor route with 8–12 distinct, stable locations. Use neutral study material such as a list or sequence; do not use the method to store passwords, private data or safety-critical instructions.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Walk the route mentally in a fixed direction and name each location in order. 2. Assign one information item to each location. 3. Create a distinctive, concrete image that links the item to the location; exaggeration may aid distinctiveness but should not obscure the item. 4. Keep one item per locus during initial learning. 5. Mentally walk the route and retrieve each item without looking at the list. 6. Check exact wording or meaning against the source. 7. Repair weak loci with a more distinctive association. 8. Practise forward retrieval first, then selected reverse or random retrieval only when ordered recall is stable.

**Practice dose:** Build one short palace in 10–20 minutes. Reuse a stable route for practice, but do not overload it with unrelated lists before the first set is retained.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Recall at least 9 of 10 items in correct order immediately and after a delay, with meanings accurate rather than merely recalling the associated image. Record intrusions and order swaps.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: choosing locations that are too similar; creating elaborate images that displace the target; placing several items at one locus too early; and confusing vivid imagery with accurate recall.

**Delayed retention check:** Test after 24 hours and again after one week. If a locus fails, revise that association and retest; preserve earlier evidence.

**Transfer task:** Use the method for a presentation outline or ordered study list, then explain the material without relying on the palace.

**Safety and scope:** Do not encode credentials, PINs, private personal information or emergency procedures in place of secure storage or formal training.

**Evidence to record:** Route map, item-to-locus mapping, immediate and delayed recall scores, intrusion/order errors.

**Status:** Draft authored; review requirements remain open.

## MS-15-004 — Lucid-dream awareness — sleep-safe reflection

**Target performance:** Maintain a brief, non-disruptive dream journal and practise reality-check reflection without reducing sleep, inducing sleep paralysis or using unsafe sleep manipulation.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Sleep duration and regularity take priority. This is an optional self-observation activity, not a health treatment or guaranteed way to induce lucid dreams. Do not practise if it increases anxiety, nightmares or sleep disruption.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Keep a notebook or private note available for use after waking, without setting alarms to interrupt sleep. 2. On waking naturally, record remembered dream fragments, emotions and uncertainty; do not invent missing details. 3. During daytime, occasionally pause and ask what evidence shows you are awake, checking stable context and memory rather than relying on one arbitrary test. 4. Record the context and result of the reflection. 5. Review journal entries for recurring themes without treating dreams as predictions or diagnoses. 6. Stop the practice if it makes sleep worse, creates distress or blurs waking and dream memories.

**Practice dose:** A brief morning note and a few daytime reflections are sufficient. Never sacrifice sleep time or deliberately interrupt sleep for practice.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** The skill target is consistent, calm recording and reality-based reflection—not achieving a lucid dream. Pass when journaling remains accurate, sleep routine is preserved and the learner can clearly distinguish dream memory from waking evidence.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: sleep deprivation; repeated night alarms; interpreting dreams as factual predictions; confusing memory reconstruction with observation; and becoming distressed by the practice.

**Delayed retention check:** Review after one week for sleep quality and distress. Continue only if neutral or beneficial; discontinue if sleep or wellbeing worsens.

**Transfer task:** Use the journal to practise careful observation and uncertainty, not to make decisions about health, relationships or future events.

**Safety and scope:** Stop if the activity disrupts sleep, increases anxiety or makes waking/dream distinctions distressing; never use sleep deprivation or night alarms.

**Evidence to record:** Brief journal entries, sleep-routine adherence self-check, distress/impact note and stop/continue decision.

**Status:** Draft authored; review requirements remain open.

## MS-15-005 — Speed Reading — flexible reading-rate control

**Target performance:** Adjust reading speed to purpose while maintaining adequate comprehension, recall and critical evaluation; increase speed only where the text and task permit.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use non-fiction passages at a known difficulty. Establish a baseline using both reading time and comprehension. Do not equate rapid page turning with reading proficiency.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Choose a passage and state the reading purpose (overview, locate a fact, understand an argument or study deeply). 2. Record baseline time and answer comprehension questions without looking back. 3. Practise previewing headings, structure and diagrams before reading. 4. Use faster scanning only to locate a named detail or identify section relevance. 5. For explanatory paragraphs, read at a pace that preserves causal links, definitions and qualifications. 6. Pause to paraphrase each section from memory. 7. Check answers and note where faster pace caused omissions. 8. Select a suitable pace for the next text based on task and difficulty, not a universal target.

**Practice dose:** Use two short passages per session: one for scanning and one for deep comprehension. Keep difficulty comparable when comparing speed.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** For scanning, locate specified facts accurately. For deep reading, meet a pre-set comprehension threshold (for example 4/5 questions) and produce a faithful summary. Speed gains count only if comprehension remains stable.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: suppressing comprehension to increase words per minute; skipping negations or qualifications; using a finger or pointer as a magical speed aid; and applying scanning to complex learning material.

**Delayed retention check:** Repeat a matched-difficulty passage after several days and compare both time and comprehension.

**Transfer task:** Use scanning to triage a long article, then slow down for the key evidence and write a source-faithful summary.

**Safety and scope:** No special physical risk. Do not use speed-reading claims to bypass careful reading of contracts, safety instructions, medical information or technical documentation.

**Evidence to record:** Passage difficulty, purpose, timed reading, comprehension answers, summary quality and delayed comparison.

**Status:** Draft authored; review requirements remain open.

## MS-15-006 — Morse Code — basic send and receive

**Target performance:** Recognise and produce a defined beginner set of Morse letters and numbers with consistent timing, then decode short messages without relying solely on visual lookup.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use an accurate International Morse reference and a simple audio or keying tool. Start with a small character set and distinguish sound rhythm from written dot/dash symbols.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Learn a small group of characters with clearly different rhythms. 2. Listen to each character at a comfortable character speed with generous spacing between characters. 3. Identify the sound pattern before looking at the written code. 4. Practise sending by tapping or typing with consistent short and long elements and clear gaps. 5. Mix learned characters into random sequences so order cannot be memorised. 6. Add new characters in small groups and interleave old ones. 7. Decode short words, then short sentences, checking every character. 8. Record confusions (such as similar rhythm patterns) and practise them in contrast pairs.

**Practice dose:** 5–10 minutes per session, several days per week. Use short, accurate practice rather than long fatigue sessions.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Decode at least 90% of characters in an unseen beginner set and send a short phrase with consistent timing and unambiguous spacing. Report the character set and speed used; do not claim general fluency from one short test.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: learning only visual dots and dashes without sound; inconsistent element length; gaps that blur letters and words; memorising a fixed sequence; and adding speed before accuracy.

**Delayed retention check:** Test an unseen sequence after 48 hours and again after a week. Increase speed only if accuracy remains stable.

**Transfer task:** Send and decode a short non-sensitive practice message with a partner, then explain why timing conventions matter.

**Safety and scope:** Use only legal, authorised communication channels and permitted equipment. Do not transmit on regulated radio frequencies without required authorisation.

**Evidence to record:** Audio practice set, character accuracy, sending sample, timing conditions, confusion list and delayed unseen test.

**Status:** Draft authored; review requirements remain open.

## MS-15-007 — Rubik’s Cube 3×3 — beginner solve

**Target performance:** Solve a standard 3×3 cube using a beginner layer-by-layer method, explaining the purpose of each stage and recovering from common setup mistakes.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a functioning 3×3 cube and one consistent beginner method. Learn notation before algorithms; check that the cube is not physically disassembled or misassembled.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Learn face notation and how turns are described from the solver’s perspective. 2. Build the first-layer cross while matching side-centre colours. 3. Insert first-layer corners using a repeatable trigger and verify the layer. 4. Solve middle-layer edges, learning left/right insertion cases. 5. Form the last-layer cross and orient the last-layer edges. 6. Position last-layer corners, then orient them using the method’s exact setup and repetition rules. 7. Finish by checking every face and correcting any misapplied algorithm. 8. Practise recognising cases before turning; say the stage aloud during early attempts.

**Practice dose:** Practise 10–20 minutes at a time with breaks. Learn one stage at a time; do not chase solve time until solves are accurate and repeatable.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Complete three solves on separate attempts using the same method, with no external step-by-step lookup during the solve. Explain the stage sequence and identify one error case. Timing is secondary and recorded only after consistency.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: losing cube orientation; confusing clockwise direction from the face being turned; memorising algorithms without recognising the case; damaging solved layers through incorrect setup; and trying to speedsolve too early.

**Delayed retention check:** Complete a cold solve after several days. If a stage fails, practise case recognition and that stage separately before another full solve.

**Transfer task:** Teach the method to another beginner or solve from a different starting scramble while preserving the stage logic.

**Safety and scope:** Avoid excessive repetitive gripping if fingers or wrists become sore; stop and rest.

**Evidence to record:** Solve records, stage errors, external prompts used, time if relevant and delayed cold solve.

**Status:** Draft authored; review requirements remain open.

## MS-15-008 — Locksport fundamentals — owned transparent practice lock only

**Target performance:** Understand the basic pin-tumbler demonstration model and practise lawful, non-destructive observation on a learner-owned transparent educational practice lock.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use only a transparent training lock personally owned or explicitly authorised for training. No real doors, padlocks in use, property, unknown locks, bypassing or entry attempts. Obtain adult oversight where appropriate.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Identify the visible plug, shell, keyway, pins and springs on the transparent model using its manufacturer’s educational guide. 2. Observe how the correct key aligns the model’s pin stacks at the shear line. 3. Insert and remove the supplied key slowly and note the visible changes. 4. Compare a correct key with a deliberately non-matching training key supplied with the kit, if available, and observe why the plug does not turn. 5. Describe the mechanism using a labelled diagram. 6. Follow only the training kit’s intended demonstration activity; do not transfer practice to real locks. 7. Return all tools and parts to storage and document what the model does not represent accurately.

**Practice dose:** Short supervised sessions of 5–10 minutes. This is a mechanical literacy exercise, not a goal to open real locks or develop covert entry capability.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Explain the parts and shear-line principle using the transparent model, correctly distinguish authorised practice from unauthorised access, and demonstrate responsible handling and storage. No real-lock opening test is part of this protocol.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: assuming a transparent model perfectly represents real tolerances; treating possession of a tool as permission; practising on locks in service; and interpreting success as authorisation.

**Delayed retention check:** After a week, reproduce the labelled diagram and explain the permission boundary without prompting.

**Transfer task:** Transfer the understanding to a general explanation of mechanical tolerances or security design, not to opening a real lock.

**Safety and scope:** Strict legal/ethical boundary: learner-owned transparent educational practice lock only. Never attempt to open, manipulate or bypass any real lock or property without explicit authorisation. Do not carry tools for this purpose.

**Evidence to record:** Ownership/permission record, labelled diagram, explanation of mechanism and boundary, supervised observation notes.

**Status:** Draft authored; review requirements remain open.

## MS-15-009 — French Drop — basic coin-vanish sleight

**Target performance:** Perform a simple coin-transfer illusion in which the audience’s attention is directed to an apparent receiving hand while the coin remains concealed in the other hand.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use one ordinary coin of a size that is comfortable to handle. Practise seated over a clear table or soft surface. Learn the movement from a reputable beginner magic source and focus on naturalness rather than speed.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Hold the coin visibly between the thumb and fingers of the dominant hand in a relaxed, repeatable position. 2. Bring the receiving hand toward the coin as if to take it, keeping the action smooth and unhurried. 3. Practise the concealed retention of the coin in the original hand slowly without performing for an audience. 4. Rehearse the receiving hand closing naturally as though it contains the coin; avoid exaggerated squeezing. 5. Coordinate gaze and head orientation with the apparent transfer only after the hand action is mechanically consistent. 6. Practise both hands separately in a mirror or video. 7. Perform the sequence for a trusted observer and ask where they believed the coin went. 8. Adjust only one variable at a time: finger tension, timing, gaze or hand path.

**Practice dose:** Use 3–5 minute sets with breaks. Stop if repetitive finger tension causes discomfort.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** In five trials for a consenting observer, the apparent transfer should be plausible in at least four, while the coin remains secure and no unnatural hand posture is visible. The learner must also be able to reveal and recover the coin safely.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: gripping too tightly; moving the hands at different speeds; looking at the concealed coin; closing the receiving hand too early; and practising only from one viewing angle.

**Delayed retention check:** Repeat after several days from a cold start and test from a second safe viewing angle.

**Transfer task:** Present the effect as entertainment and then explain that it is a sleight-of-hand illusion if asked; do not use it to deceive in transactions or manipulate someone.

**Safety and scope:** Keep coins away from young children and pets due to choking risk. Do not throw coins or practise near eyes or faces.

**Evidence to record:** Practice video or observer feedback, coin-control success, hand tension notes and delayed performance.

**Status:** Draft authored; review requirements remain open.

## MS-15-010 — One-handed card shuffle — basic packet control

**Target performance:** Perform a controlled one-handed packet shuffle with a standard deck, maintaining card order control and recovering dropped cards without force.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a standard deck in good condition and learn one beginner grip from a reputable card-handling demonstration. Start with a small packet if a full deck is too large for the hand.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Learn the grip and identify where the deck is supported by the thumb and fingers. 2. Practise separating the deck into two stable packets without bending cards sharply. 3. Move the packets through the shuffle path slowly, keeping the motion within comfortable finger range. 4. Recombine the packets and square the deck. 5. Repeat with fewer cards until the movement is controlled, then increase packet size gradually. 6. Practise above a table so dropped cards are easy to recover. 7. Record which transition causes instability and isolate that transition. 8. Perform a complete shuffle only after the component movements are repeatable.

**Practice dose:** Use 3–5 minute practice sets with rest. Stop for pain, numbness or persistent strain; hand size and mobility vary, so adaptation is acceptable.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Complete five controlled shuffles in a row without throwing cards, excessive bending or painful finger positions; square the deck cleanly and recover a dropped packet calmly. Speed is not a pass criterion.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: using force instead of alignment; trying a full deck before packet control; bending cards sharply; copying a grip that does not fit the learner’s hand; and continuing through pain.

**Delayed retention check:** Repeat a small number of cold shuffles after 48 hours. If control is inconsistent, reduce packet size and rebuild the movement.

**Transfer task:** Use the skill as a dexterity demonstration or card-game handling flourish; explain that it is a physical handling skill, not evidence of general hand superiority.

**Safety and scope:** Stop with discomfort or pain. Keep cards away from liquids and avoid sharp-edged or damaged decks.

**Evidence to record:** Packet size, successful repetitions, dropped-card frequency, comfort notes and delayed performance.
