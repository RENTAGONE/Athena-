# ATHENA Substantive Micro-skill Protocols — Batch 01

**Status:** Substantive authored drafts; independent review and calibration required. These protocols do not certify mastery.

**Scope:** Ten cognitive and learning micro-skills. This is the first actual rewrite batch; it supersedes the generic acquisition wording for these ten IDs when quality status is evaluated.

## MS-01-001 — Active recall from a blank page

**Target performance:** Reconstruct the structure and important relationships of a recently studied topic without looking at notes, then compare against a trusted source and repair omissions.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Choose a bounded topic that has already been studied; do not use a topic never understood. Prepare blank paper and the source material for later checking. Before recall, write the topic and a confidence prediction (low/medium/high).

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Close all notes and write the central question or topic title. 2. Spend 3–5 minutes drawing or listing everything remembered, grouping related ideas rather than copying remembered sentences. 3. Add arrows or short phrases showing causal, hierarchical or sequential relationships. 4. Mark uncertain items with a question mark instead of guessing confidently. 5. Open the source and compare line by line: mark accurate recall, missing essentials, distorted relationships and unsupported additions in four different ways. 6. Close the source and reconstruct only the missed or distorted parts. 7. Finish by writing two questions that would expose the most important remaining gap.

**Practice dose:** Use one 10–15 minute cycle per topic. One cycle is enough for an initial attempt; repeat only the weak sections after feedback. Do not turn the task into a speed contest.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Given a one-page topic previously studied, produce a map containing the central idea, at least five key elements and at least three correct relationships. Score each element for accuracy and each relationship for direction/meaning. Pass the initial check when all central ideas are accurate and at least 80% of essential elements and relationships are correct; verify again on a different day.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: rereading before attempting; writing disconnected keywords without relationships; treating fluent-sounding guesses as facts; copying source wording during the check; and counting page coverage rather than accuracy.

**Delayed retention check:** After 24–72 hours, perform a cold reconstruction without review immediately beforehand. If the central structure is missing or accuracy falls below the agreed threshold, mark needs_revision and target only the failed links.

**Transfer task:** Explain the same topic to a novice using the recalled map, then answer a “why does this connect?” question. Transfer is successful when the structure is preserved while wording and example change.

**Safety and scope:** Cognitive; no physical safety concern. Protect privacy if the topic contains personal or confidential information. Use a reliable source for the correction stage.

**Evidence to record:** A dated recall map, source used, marked error categories, unaided delayed map, and brief transfer explanation.

**Status:** Draft authored; subject review, example calibration and learner testing required.

## MS-01-002 — Confidence calibration

**Target performance:** Estimate the probability that an answer is correct before feedback, then compare confidence with observed accuracy over repeated, varied questions.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use practice questions with answer keys or an independent checker. Separate confidence in the answer from confidence in the explanation. Avoid using emotionally loaded or high-stakes decisions for early practice.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Answer a small set of 10 questions without checking. 2. Before revealing feedback, assign each answer a probability such as 50%, 70%, 90% or 100%; do not use “sure” without a number. 3. Record a one-line reason for high-confidence answers. 4. Check answers against the key and classify each as correct/incorrect. 5. Group answers by confidence band (for example 60–69%, 70–79%, 80–89%, 90–100%) and compare stated confidence with actual accuracy. 6. Inspect high-confidence errors first: identify missing knowledge, misread wording, faulty reasoning or unjustified assumption. 7. Repeat with a fresh set and adjust confidence estimates only when evidence supports the adjustment.

**Practice dose:** Use 10–20 questions per session, two or three sessions per topic. Avoid overinterpreting small samples; confidence calibration improves through repeated observations, not one quiz.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Across at least 30 varied items over multiple sessions, confidence should become meaningfully ordered: higher-confidence groups should generally have higher accuracy. Pass is not “100% accuracy”; it is honest estimates, fewer unjustified 95–100% claims and an ability to explain sources of miscalibration.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: changing confidence after seeing the answer; using confidence as a substitute for evidence; comparing tiny samples as if statistically stable; and lowering all confidence indiscriminately.

**Delayed retention check:** Repeat a short unseen set after one week. Compare calibration patterns, not just raw score. Keep earlier data to show whether overconfidence is changing.

**Transfer task:** Before making a low-stakes project decision, write a probability and reason, then later compare the prediction with the outcome. Do not use this practice alone for medical, legal, financial or safety-critical decisions.

**Safety and scope:** Do not use this as a diagnostic tool for mental health or as a substitute for professional risk assessment.

**Evidence to record:** Question set and answer key, pre-feedback confidence per item, correctness, confidence-band summary, error explanations and delayed comparison.

**Status:** Draft authored; subject review, example calibration and learner testing required.

## MS-01-003 — Explain a concept simply

**Target performance:** Give a concise explanation that preserves the mechanism, distinguishes examples from the rule, and survives a novice’s follow-up questions.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Select a concept the learner can already solve or use. Write its formal definition or governing model first so simplification does not become distortion.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. State the concept in one accurate sentence. 2. Identify the mechanism: what changes, what causes it, or what rule governs it. 3. Choose one familiar example that demonstrates the mechanism. 4. Explain the example in plain language without replacing technical terms that are essential. 5. State one boundary or case where the explanation would not apply. 6. Deliver a 60–90 second explanation without reading. 7. Ask a listener to paraphrase the explanation and pose two “why/how” questions. 8. Correct any misunderstanding, then deliver a cleaner second version.

**Practice dose:** Practise one concept at a time. Make two short recordings or live explanations with feedback between them; stop when voice fatigue or frustration affects clarity.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** A novice can paraphrase the central mechanism, the explanation contains no material factual error, the example genuinely fits, and the learner correctly answers two follow-up questions. A simple explanation that omits a crucial condition does not pass.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: using an analogy as if it were the mechanism; removing necessary conditions; jargon dumping; oversimplifying into a false statement; and memorising a script without understanding.

**Delayed retention check:** Explain the concept again after several days without using the previous script. Check whether the mechanism and boundary condition remain accurate.

**Transfer task:** Teach the same concept to two audiences with different prior knowledge. Change vocabulary and example, not the underlying truth.

**Safety and scope:** Use accurate, age-appropriate material. Do not present uncertain or disputed claims as settled facts; disclose uncertainty.

**Evidence to record:** Audio or observer notes, listener paraphrase, follow-up responses, factual correction log and delayed explanation.

**Status:** Draft authored; subject review, example calibration and learner testing required.

## MS-01-004 — Build a useful analogy

**Target performance:** Construct an analogy that maps at least three structural relationships between a target concept and a familiar source, then identify where the mapping breaks.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Understand the target concept sufficiently to list its components, relationships and limits. Choose a familiar source system with comparable structure, not merely a similar appearance.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Write the target concept’s key elements and how they interact. 2. Select a familiar source system. 3. Create a two-column mapping of target element to source element. 4. For each mapping, state the shared relationship (for example flow, constraint, feedback or hierarchy). 5. Test the analogy against an ordinary case and an edge case. 6. Write at least two explicit limits: what the source system does that the target does not, or vice versa. 7. Revise any mapping that causes a false inference. 8. Present the analogy and then restate the target concept without the analogy.

**Practice dose:** Build one analogy per concept in 10–20 minutes. Prefer one accurate analogy over several decorative ones.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Pass when at least three correspondences preserve relevant relationships, the analogy explains something that was initially difficult, at least two limits are explicit, and the learner can restate the real mechanism without relying on the analogy.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: matching surface features only; stretching one analogy to cover every case; forgetting that analogy is a teaching aid rather than proof; and failing to explain where the mapping breaks.

**Delayed retention check:** After a delay, explain the target concept without seeing the analogy, then reconstruct the analogy and its limits.

**Transfer task:** Use the same target concept with a different audience or a second analogy. Explain why one analogy is more useful for one aspect and less useful for another.

**Safety and scope:** No special safety concern; check that examples do not embed stereotypes or misleading claims.

**Evidence to record:** Target model, analogy mapping table, two limits, edge-case test, listener feedback and delayed restatement.

**Status:** Draft authored; subject review, example calibration and learner testing required.

## MS-01-005 — Spot a hidden assumption

**Target performance:** Identify an unstated premise that must be true for a claim or argument to work, and test whether the premise is supported.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use short, non-sensitive claims with enough context to interpret. Distinguish an assumption from a fact explicitly stated in the argument.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Rewrite the claim in neutral wording. 2. Identify the conclusion and the reasons offered for it. 3. Ask “what must also be true for these reasons to support this conclusion?” 4. Generate possible missing premises. 5. Select the premise most necessary to the inference, not merely an interesting background belief. 6. Test it with a counterexample: if the premise were false, would the argument weaken? 7. Classify the premise as supported, unsupported, contested or unnecessary. 8. Rewrite the argument with the premise made explicit.

**Practice dose:** Analyse three to five short arguments per session. Include arguments that are sound as well as weak ones to prevent reflexive fault-finding.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** Given five arguments, identify a relevant necessary or highly material assumption in at least four, explain why it matters, and distinguish it from a merely possible assumption.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: inventing motives; confusing an assumption with a conclusion; finding irrelevant background conditions; and treating every unstated premise as automatically false.

**Delayed retention check:** Use a fresh argument after several days. Score relevance and explanatory justification, not the number of assumptions generated.

**Transfer task:** Apply the method to a product claim, a school explanation or a project proposal. Seek evidence for the premise before accepting or rejecting it.

**Safety and scope:** Avoid personal attacks. Critique reasoning and evidence rather than inferring hidden intent or character.

**Evidence to record:** Annotated arguments, identified premises, counterexample tests, support classification and delayed cold analysis.

**Status:** Draft authored; subject review, example calibration and learner testing required.

## MS-01-006 — Separate fact, inference, and opinion

**Target performance:** Classify statements by what is directly observed or verifiable, what is inferred from evidence, and what expresses evaluation or preference.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a short article, report or everyday scenario with traceable source material. Recognise that a sentence can contain more than one claim and may need splitting.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Break compound sentences into individual claims. 2. Mark direct observations or independently checkable records as factual claims, while noting that the source may still be wrong. 3. Mark conclusions that go beyond the observation as inferences and write the evidence supporting them. 4. Mark value judgements or preferences as opinions and identify the value standard behind them. 5. Flag predictions, causal claims and uncertain statements separately when the three-way label is insufficient. 6. Find one statement whose label changes when more evidence is supplied. 7. Rewrite ambiguous statements to show what is known and what is inferred.

**Practice dose:** Classify 10–15 statements in a session. Discuss borderline cases rather than forcing certainty.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** At least 12 of 15 statements are classified with defensible reasons; compound statements are split; and uncertain or predictive claims are not forced into a simplistic category.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: assuming “fact” means true; assuming opinions cannot be evidence-informed; labelling all interpretations as opinions; and ignoring the source or measurement process.

**Delayed retention check:** Reclassify a new passage after a delay. Include at least two ambiguous cases and justify uncertainty.

**Transfer task:** Use the framework when reading a product review or news claim; trace one factual claim to its source and label your own inference separately.

**Safety and scope:** Do not expose private information in examples. Preserve context and avoid presenting allegations as facts.

**Evidence to record:** Passage annotations, source links, reasons for classifications, uncertainty flags and delayed classification result.

**Status:** Draft authored; subject review, example calibration and learner testing required.

## MS-01-007 — Create a retrieval prompt

**Target performance:** Convert a learning objective into questions that require retrieval and reasoning rather than recognition or copying.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Start from a clear objective and the material it refers to. Identify what the learner should recall, explain, perform, compare or transfer.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Rewrite the objective as an observable action. 2. Draft one free-recall prompt with no answer cues. 3. Draft one explanation prompt asking why or how. 4. Draft one application prompt using a changed example. 5. Draft one discrimination prompt asking when a rule does not apply. 6. Draft one transfer prompt that changes context or constraints. 7. Check that each prompt tests the objective rather than trivia. 8. Write a concise answer key or rubric, including acceptable variants and common misconceptions. 9. Pilot the prompts without notes and revise questions that are ambiguous or too easy to guess.

**Practice dose:** Create a five-prompt set per learning objective. Keep prompts short enough to answer, but do not make them so leading that they reveal the answer.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** A reviewer can identify the target objective for every prompt; the set covers recall, explanation, application, discrimination and transfer; and the answer guide distinguishes correct reasoning from keyword matching.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: writing recognition-only multiple choice; asking vague “explain everything” questions; embedding clues; and creating prompts without a scoring criterion.

**Delayed retention check:** Use the prompts after a delay and record which prompt exposes a genuine gap. Revise prompts that repeatedly produce false positives.

**Transfer task:** Adapt one prompt to a new real-world scenario while preserving the underlying objective.

**Safety and scope:** Use accurate answer keys and disclose uncertainty where content is contested.

**Evidence to record:** Objective, five prompts, answer guide/rubric, pilot responses, revision notes and delayed performance.

**Status:** Draft authored; subject review, example calibration and learner testing required.

## MS-01-008 — Error log analysis

**Target performance:** Turn repeated mistakes into a specific diagnosis and a targeted corrective practice rather than merely recording that an answer was wrong.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use a recent exercise, coding task or project with enough work shown to trace the error. Avoid recording passwords or sensitive personal data.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Capture the original task and the exact point of failure. 2. Preserve the attempted reasoning or steps before correcting it. 3. Classify the error: knowledge gap, misinterpretation, procedure, calculation, attention, tool/environment, or strategy. 4. Ask what evidence distinguishes the chosen category from alternatives. 5. Identify the earliest incorrect step, not only the final wrong answer. 6. Write a corrective rule or test that would catch this error next time. 7. Create one near-transfer practice item targeting the same failure mechanism. 8. Reattempt without looking at the correction and compare.

**Practice dose:** Log only meaningful errors; review 3–5 at a time. A large log without diagnosis or action is not useful.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** For at least four recent errors, the learner identifies the earliest faulty step, gives a defensible cause, chooses a targeted correction, and demonstrates improvement on a new item of the same type.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: writing “careless mistake” without evidence; fixing the final answer but not the reasoning; overgeneralising from one incident; and repeatedly practising the whole topic instead of the failed subskill.

**Delayed retention check:** Review the log after one week. Check whether the same error recurs and whether the corrective rule catches it. Preserve recurrence history.

**Transfer task:** Use the error taxonomy in a different domain, such as moving from math errors to debugging, while adapting the categories to the task.

**Safety and scope:** Keep logs private where appropriate; do not store confidential code, account details or personal data.

**Evidence to record:** Original attempt, error location, classification and rationale, corrective test, new-item result and recurrence record.

**Status:** Draft authored; subject review, example calibration and learner testing required.

## MS-01-009 — Estimate before calculating

**Target performance:** Produce a reasonable magnitude or range before exact calculation, then use the estimate to detect implausible results.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Use calculations where units and approximate magnitudes are understood. State assumptions; do not estimate in place of exact work where safety or precision requirements demand exact measurement.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Read the problem and identify the quantity being asked for. 2. Write the unit and expected scale (ones, tens, hundreds, etc.). 3. Round inputs to convenient values while preserving the order of magnitude. 4. Calculate a rough result mentally or with simple arithmetic. 5. Record an interval if uncertainty is meaningful. 6. Perform the exact calculation separately. 7. Compare exact result with the estimate and explain any large discrepancy. 8. Check units and whether the result makes sense in the original context.

**Practice dose:** Use five varied problems per session: whole-number arithmetic, decimals/percentages, ratios, measurement and real-life quantities. Keep estimates distinct from exact answers.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** For at least four of five tasks, the estimate is within a pre-agreed reasonable range, the unit is correct, and the learner can use the estimate to catch an intentionally planted implausible answer.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: rounding too aggressively; losing units; estimating after seeing the exact answer; using a single point estimate when a range is more honest; and confusing estimation with guessing.

**Delayed retention check:** Repeat with unseen problems after a delay. Include a problem where rounding in the wrong direction creates a misleading estimate.

**Transfer task:** Use estimation to check a budget, travel time or project material quantity; then verify with exact data before acting.

**Safety and scope:** For financial, construction, medical or electrical decisions, estimation is only a sanity check and must not replace required exact calculation or qualified verification.

**Evidence to record:** Written estimate made before exact work, units, assumptions, exact result, discrepancy explanation and delayed unseen result.

**Status:** Draft authored; subject review, example calibration and learner testing required.

## MS-01-010 — Chunk a procedure

**Target performance:** Group a multi-step procedure into meaningful units that preserve order, decision points and error checks, then reconstruct it without losing critical steps.

**Prerequisites and setup:** Choose a procedure with a clear start and finish, such as a software workflow, study routine or safe household task. Do not compress safety-critical instructions in ways that remove warnings.

**Acquisition sequence:**

1. Perform or inspect the full procedure once and list every action in order. 2. Mark decision points, dependencies and steps where errors are costly. 3. Group adjacent steps that serve one purpose into named chunks. 4. Give each chunk a short label describing its outcome, not just its first action. 5. Write the trigger for moving to the next chunk and any verification check. 6. Test the chunking by explaining the full sequence from the labels alone. 7. Reopen the detailed steps and check that no exception, warning or dependency was lost. 8. Practise the chunks in order, then vary one non-critical condition and explain what changes.

**Practice dose:** Chunk one procedure at a time. Aim for a small number of meaningful groups; do not force a fixed chunk count across different tasks.

**Assessment and pass criterion:** A learner can reconstruct the complete procedure from chunk labels, preserve all critical decision points and safety checks, and explain when a chunk cannot be skipped.

**Common failure modes:** Common errors: arbitrary grouping by equal step count; removing exceptions; making labels too vague; treating a mnemonic as a substitute for understanding; and chunking a procedure never understood.

**Delayed retention check:** Reconstruct the procedure after a delay from chunk labels, then compare with the authoritative procedure. Any omitted critical step requires relearning.

**Transfer task:** Apply the chunking method to a different procedure and compare which grouping principles transfer and which are domain-specific.

**Safety and scope:** For medical, electrical, workshop or emergency procedures, never simplify away warnings or replace certified instruction. Use the authoritative procedure as source of truth.

**Evidence to record:** Original ordered steps, chunk map, decision/checkpoint list, cold reconstruction, source comparison and transfer attempt.

**Status:** Draft authored; subject review, example calibration and learner testing required.
